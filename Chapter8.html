Chapter 8: Fluid Mechanics

Welcome to Fluid Mechanics

Fluids — liquids and gases — are the matter that makes up all of the world around us, from the blood in our veins to the air in our atmosphere and the fuel in our rockets. Fluid mechanics asks how forces are transmitted through these deformable, compressible materials.

Density and pressure lead to buoyant forces, which in turn connect to gravity and acceleration. The study of moving fluids then unifies macroscopic fluid behavior with conservation laws through the equation of continuity and Bernoulli's equation, giving a bridge from mechanics to energy conservation.

The final part of the chapter treats bulk matter through stress and strain, the language of elasticity used in engineering and materials science.

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Understand the concepts of density and pressure in fluids
  • Calculate pressure variations with depth in static fluids
  • Apply Archimedes' principle to solve buoyancy problems
  • Understand and apply the equation of continuity for fluid flow
  • Use Bernoulli's equation to analyze fluid dynamics problems
  • Differentiate between different types of elastic moduli
  • Calculate stress, strain, and elastic moduli for various materials
  • Apply fluid mechanics principles to real-world situations

8.1 Density and Pressure in Static Fluids

8.1.1 Density

Definition

Density (\(\rho\)) is defined as mass per unit volume:

\(\rho = \frac{m}{V}\)

where:

  • \(\rho\) = density (kg/m³)
  • \(m\) = mass (kg)
  • \(V\) = volume (m³)

The SI unit of density is kg/m³.

Key Concept

Key points about density:

  • Density is an intensive property (doesn't depend on amount)
  • Density decreases with temperature for most substances
  • Density of water at 4°C is 1000 kg/m³
  • Specific gravity = density relative to water (dimensionless)
Example

Calculate the mass of 2 m³ of water (density = 1000 kg/m³).

Solution
\(m = \rho V = (1000\,\text{kg/m}^3)(2\,\text{m}^3) = 2000\,\text{kg}\)

8.1.2 Pressure

Definition

Pressure (\(P\)) is defined as force per unit area:

\(P = \frac{F}{A}\)

where:

  • \(P\) = pressure (Pa or N/m²)
  • \(F\) = force (N)
  • \(A\) = area (m²)

1 pascal (Pa) = 1 N/m². Other common units: atm, mmHg, psi.

Key Concept

Key points about pressure:

  • Pressure is a scalar quantity (no direction)
  • Pressure acts equally in all directions in a fluid
  • Atmospheric pressure at sea level ≈ 101.3 kPa = 1 atm
  • Pressure is transmitted undiminished throughout a fluid (Pascal's principle)
Example

A force of 500 N is applied to an area of 0.2 m². Calculate the pressure.

Solution
\(P = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{500\,\text{N}}{0.2\,\text{m}^2} = 2500\,\text{Pa}\)

8.1.3 Variation of Pressure with Depth

Definition

The pressure at depth \(h\) in a fluid of density \(\rho\) is:

\(P = P_0 + \rho gh\)

where:

  • \(P\) = pressure at depth \(h\) (Pa)
  • \(P_0\) = pressure at surface (Pa)
  • \(\rho\) = fluid density (kg/m³)
  • \(g\) = acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s²)
  • \(h\) = depth below surface (m)
Key Concept

Key points about pressure variation:

  • Pressure increases linearly with depth
  • Pressure depends only on depth, not on container shape
  • Pressure is the same at all points at the same depth
  • The pressure difference between two points is \(\Delta P = \rho g \Delta h\)
Figure 8.1: Pressure variation with depth in a fluid
Example

Calculate the pressure at a depth of 10 m in water. (Atmospheric pressure = 101.3 kPa, \(\rho_{\text{water}}\) = 1000 kg/m³)

Solution
\(P = P_0 + \rho gh = 101300 + (1000)(9.8)(10) = 101300 + 98000 = 199300\,\text{Pa}\)

8.1.4 Pressure Measurements

Definition

Manometers and barometers are devices used to measure pressure:

  • Open-tube manometer: Measures gauge pressure \(P - P_{\text{atm}}\)
  • Barometer: Measures atmospheric pressure
  • Bourdon gauge: Mechanical pressure gauge
Common Pressure Units
  • 1 atmosphere (atm) = 101.3 kPa = 760 mmHg
  • 1 bar = 100 kPa
  • 1 torr = 1 mmHg
  • 1 psi = 6895 Pa
Example

A mercury barometer reads 760 mmHg. What is the atmospheric pressure in pascals? (\(\rho_{Hg}\) = 13600 kg/m³)

Solution
\(P = \rho gh = (13600)(9.8)(0.760) = 101292.8\,\text{Pa} \approx 101.3\,\text{kPa}\)

8.1.5 Pascal's Principle and Hydraulics

Pascal's principle states that a pressure change applied to an enclosed, incompressible fluid is transmitted undiminished to every part of the fluid. In a hydraulic system with two pistons:

\(P_1 = P_2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2}\)

Thus a small force on a small piston can produce a large force on a large piston. The trade-off is distance: the small piston must move farther than the large one because the volume of fluid displaced is the same:

\(A_1d_1 = A_2d_2\)

Hydraulic car lifts, excavators, and vehicle brakes exploit this principle.

Example

A hydraulic lift has a small piston of area \(0.010\,\text{m}^2\) and a large piston of area \(0.50\,\text{m}^2\). A force of \(200\,\text{N}\) is applied to the small piston.

  1. What force is exerted on the large piston?
  2. How far must the small piston move to raise the large piston by \(0.10\,\text{m}\)?
Solution

(a) Using Pascal's principle:

\(F_2 = F_1\frac{A_2}{A_1} = 200\frac{0.50}{0.010} = 10{,}000\,\text{N}\)

(b) The displaced volumes are equal, so:

\(d_1 = d_2\frac{A_2}{A_1} = (0.10)\frac{0.50}{0.010} = 5.0\,\text{m}\)

The force is multiplied by 50, but the small piston must move 50 times farther. Energy is conserved: \(F_1d_1 = F_2d_2\).

8.2 Buoyant Forces and Archimedes' Principle

Definition

Archimedes' Principle: The buoyant force on an object immersed in a fluid equals the weight of the fluid displaced by the object.

\(F_b = \rho_{\text{fluid}} V_{\text{displaced}} g\)

where:

  • \(F_b\) = buoyant force (N)
  • \(\rho_{\text{fluid}}\) = density of fluid (kg/m³)
  • \(V_{\text{displaced}}\) = volume of fluid displaced (m³)
  • \(g\) = acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s²)
Key Concept

Key points about buoyancy:

  • Buoyant force acts upward through the center of buoyancy
  • Objects float when \(F_b \geq mg\) (weight)
  • Objects sink when \(F_b < mg\)
  • Apparent weight = actual weight − buoyant force
Figure 8.2: Buoyant force and Archimedes' principle
Example

A 2 m³ object is completely submerged in water. Calculate the buoyant force. (\(\rho_{\text{water}}\) = 1000 kg/m³)

Solution
\(F_b = \rho_{\text{fluid}} V g = (1000)(2)(9.8) = 19600\,\text{N}\)
Practice Problem

A 500 kg object has a volume of 0.8 m³. Will it float or sink in water?

Solution

Maximum buoyant force:

\(F_b = \rho V g = (1000)(0.8)(9.8) = 7840\,\text{N}\)

Weight:

\(mg = (500)(9.8) = 4900\,\text{N}\)

Since \(F_b > mg\), the object will float.

Example

A steel ball of mass \(5\,\text{kg}\) is lowered into a tank of water. Estimate its volume and determine whether it will float or sink. (Use \(\rho_{\text{water}} = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3\) and \(g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2\).)

Solution

The ball's volume follows from its density. Steel has a density of about \(8000\,\text{kg/m}^3\), so

\(V = \frac{m}{\rho_{\text{steel}}} = \frac{5}{8000} = 6.25 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^3\)

The buoyant force from the water is therefore

\(F_b = \rho_{\text{fluid}} V g = (1000)(6.25 \times 10^{-4})(9.8) = 6.1\,\text{N}\)

while the ball's weight is

\(mg = (5)(9.8) = 49\,\text{N}\)

Because the weight greatly exceeds the buoyant force (\(mg > F_b\)), the ball will sink. A steel object must displace its own weight in water before it can float.

8.3 Moving Fluids and Bernoulli's Equation

8.3.1 Equation of Continuity

Definition

Equation of Continuity: For an incompressible fluid in steady flow, the mass flow rate is constant:

\(A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2\)

where:

  • \(A\) = cross-sectional area (m²)
  • \(v\) = flow velocity (m/s)

The product \(Av\) is the volume flow rate.

Key Concept

Key points about continuity:

  • Fluid speeds up when it enters a constriction
  • Flow rate is constant for incompressible fluids
  • Mass conservation leads to the continuity equation
  • The equation assumes steady, incompressible flow
Figure 8.3: Equation of continuity for fluid flow
Example

Water flows through a pipe with cross-sectional area 0.1 m² at 2 m/s. If the pipe narrows to 0.05 m², what is the flow velocity in the narrow section?

Solution
\(A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2\)
\((0.1)(2) = (0.05)v_2\)
\(0.2 = 0.05v_2 \;\Rightarrow\; v_2 = 4\,\text{m/s}\)

8.3.2 Bernoulli's Equation

Definition

Bernoulli's Equation: For an ideal fluid in steady flow:

\(P_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_1^2 + \rho g h_1 = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2 + \rho g h_2\)

or

\(P + \frac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho g h = \text{constant}\)

where:

  • \(P\) = pressure (Pa)
  • \(\rho\) = density (kg/m³)
  • \(v\) = velocity (m/s)
  • \(h\) = height (m)
  • \(g\) = acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s²)
Key Concept

Key points about Bernoulli's equation:

  • Pressure decreases where velocity increases
  • Represents conservation of energy for fluids
  • Applies to ideal fluids (incompressible, non-viscous, steady flow)
  • Used to explain lift, venturi effect, etc.
Example

Water flows through a horizontal pipe. At point 1, the pressure is 200 kPa and velocity is 2 m/s. At point 2, the velocity is 4 m/s. Calculate the pressure at point 2. (\(\rho_{\text{water}}\) = 1000 kg/m³)

Solution

Since the pipe is horizontal, \(h_1 = h_2\):

\(P_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_1^2 = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2\)
\(200000 + \frac{1}{2}(1000)(2)^2 = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}(1000)(4)^2\)
\(200000 + 2000 = P_2 + 8000\)
\(202000 = P_2 + 8000 \;\Rightarrow\; P_2 = 194000\,\text{Pa} = 194\,\text{kPa}\)

8.3.3 Applications of Bernoulli's Equation

Bernoulli's equation explains several familiar effects. In a horizontal pipe, a constriction increases the speed and lowers the pressure, which is the basis of the Venturi meter. On an aircraft wing, the air moving over the curved upper surface travels faster than the air below, so the pressure above is lower and a net upward lift results. The same principle makes a shower curtain pull inward and allows a fast-moving train to draw nearby objects toward it.

Torricelli's theorem describes efflux from a tank. If a small hole is a distance \(h\) below the free surface, the pressure at both the surface and the exit is atmospheric, and the surface speed is negligible. Bernoulli's equation then gives:

\(v = \sqrt{2gh}\)

The liquid leaves the hole with the same speed it would have after falling freely through the height \(h\).

Example

A water tank has a small hole \(5.0\,\text{m}\) below the water surface. Find the speed at which water exits the hole.

Solution

Using Torricelli's theorem:

\(v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2(9.8)(5.0)} = \sqrt{98} \approx 9.9\,\text{m/s}\)

The result is independent of the tank's shape and of the water density, just as free-fall speed is independent of mass.

8.4 Properties of Bulk Matter: Stress and Strain

8.4.1 Young's Modulus: Elasticity in Length

Definition

Young's Modulus (\(Y\)) measures a material's resistance to linear deformation:

\(Y = \frac{\text{stress}}{\text{strain}} = \frac{F/A}{\Delta L/L_0}\)

where:

  • \(F\) = applied force (N)
  • \(A\) = cross-sectional area (m²)
  • \(\Delta L\) = change in length (m)
  • \(L_0\) = original length (m)

Units: Pa or N/m²

Key Concept

Key points about Young's modulus:

  • Measures stiffness of materials
  • Large \(Y\) = stiff material (hard to deform)
  • Small \(Y\) = flexible material (easy to deform)
  • Applies to tensile and compressive stresses
Example

A steel wire (\(Y = 2.0 \times 10^{11}\) Pa) of length 2 m and cross-sectional area 0.1 cm² stretches by 1 mm under a load. Calculate the force applied.

Solution
\(Y = \frac{F/A}{\Delta L/L_0}\)
\(F = Y A \frac{\Delta L}{L_0} = (2.0 \times 10^{11})(1 \times 10^{-5})\left(\frac{0.001}{2}\right) = 1000\,\text{N}\)

8.4.2 Shear Modulus: Elasticity of Shape

Definition

Shear Modulus (\(G\)) measures a material's resistance to shearing deformation:

\(G = \frac{\text{shear stress}}{\text{shear strain}} = \frac{F/A}{\Delta x/h}\)

where:

  • \(F\) = tangential force (N)
  • \(A\) = area (m²)
  • \(\Delta x\) = lateral displacement (m)
  • \(h\) = height (m)

Units: Pa or N/m²

Key Concept

Key points about shear modulus:

  • Measures resistance to shape change
  • Applies to forces parallel to surface
  • Related to material's rigidity
  • Zero for fluids (they flow under shear stress)
Example

A tangential force of \(500\,\text{N}\) is applied to the top surface of a block of area \(2.0\times10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2\). The top surface is displaced \(0.50\,\text{cm}\) relative to the bottom, and the block is \(10\,\text{cm}\) tall. Find the shear modulus.

Solution

The shear stress is:

\(\frac{F}{A} = \frac{500}{2.0\times10^{-4}} = 2.5\times10^6\,\text{Pa}\)

The shear strain is:

\(\frac{\Delta x}{h} = \frac{0.0050}{0.10} = 0.050\)

Therefore:

\(G = \frac{2.5\times10^6}{0.050} = 5.0\times10^7\,\text{Pa}\)

This value is typical of a fairly rigid solid; fluids have no static shear modulus because they deform continuously under shear.

8.4.3 Bulk Modulus: Volume Elasticity

Definition

Bulk Modulus (\(B\)) measures a material's resistance to volume change:

\(B = \frac{\text{volume stress}}{\text{volume strain}} = \frac{\Delta P}{-\Delta V/V_0}\)

where:

  • \(\Delta P\) = pressure change (Pa)
  • \(\Delta V\) = volume change (m³)
  • \(V_0\) = original volume (m³)

The negative sign indicates that pressure increase causes volume decrease.

Key Concept

Key points about bulk modulus:

  • Measures compressibility of materials
  • Large \(B\) = incompressible material
  • Small \(B\) = compressible material
  • Reciprocal of bulk modulus is compressibility
Example

A volume of water decreases by 0.01% when the pressure is increased by 2 atm. Calculate the bulk modulus of water.

Solution
\(B = \frac{\Delta P}{-\Delta V/V_0} = \frac{2 \times 101325}{0.0001} = \frac{202650}{0.0001} = 2.0265 \times 10^9\,\text{Pa}\)

Problem-Solving Workshop

Fluid problems combine geometry, mass conservation, and energy conservation. Start with a sketch of the pipe or object, label cross-sectional areas, heights, pressures, and velocities, and convert every quantity to SI units before substituting.

For steady, incompressible flow, continuity fixes the relationship between speeds at two sections. Bernoulli's equation then connects pressure, speed, and elevation along a streamline. In a horizontal Venturi meter the height terms cancel, so a measured pressure drop directly determines the speed change.

Problem-Solving Checklist
  1. Sketch the pipe or object and label two convenient sections with \(A\), \(v\), \(P\), and \(h\).
  2. Convert areas to m², pressures to Pa, and densities to kg/m³ before calculating.
  3. Use continuity \(A_1v_1 = A_2v_2\) to express one speed in terms of the other.
  4. Apply Bernoulli's equation along a streamline; cancel height terms when the pipe is horizontal.
  5. Solve for the unknown speed or pressure, then compute the volume flow rate \(Q = Av\).
  6. Check that the fluid speeds up at the narrower section and that the final units are correct.
Worked Example

Water flows through a horizontal Venturi tube. The wide section has cross-sectional area \(8.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^2\), and the throat has area \(2.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^2\). The pressure difference between the wide section and the throat is \(15\,\text{kPa}\). Taking \(\rho = 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3\), find the speed at the wide section, the speed at the throat, and the volume flow rate.

Solution

From continuity, the speed at the throat is larger by the area ratio:

\(v_2 = \frac{A_1}{A_2}v_1 = \frac{8.0 \times 10^{-3}}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}}v_1 = 4v_1\)

For horizontal flow, the height terms in Bernoulli's equation cancel, leaving:

\(P_1 - P_2 = \frac{1}{2}\rho(v_2^2 - v_1^2)\)

Convert the pressure difference to pascals and substitute \(v_2 = 4v_1\):

\(15{,}000 = \frac{1}{2}(1000)\left[(4v_1)^2 - v_1^2\right] = 500(15v_1^2) = 7500v_1^2\)
\(v_1^2 = \frac{15{,}000}{7500} = 2.00 \;\Rightarrow\; v_1 \approx 1.41\,\text{m/s}\)
\(v_2 = 4(1.41) \approx 5.66\,\text{m/s}\)

The volume flow rate is:

\(Q = A_1v_1 = (8.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^2)(1.41\,\text{m/s}) \approx 1.13 \times 10^{-2}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}\)

This is about \(11\,\text{L/s}\), and the corresponding mass flow rate is \(\rho Q \approx 11.3\,\text{kg/s}\). The throat speed is greater and the throat pressure is lower, as Bernoulli's equation predicts.

Why This Matters

Venturi meters use exactly this pressure drop to measure the flow of water, fuel, or air in industrial piping and in carburetors. The same principle explains why an aspirator or perfume atomizer draws fluid into a fast-moving stream, and it guides the design of aircraft and HVAC ducts.

Key Takeaways

  • Density is mass per unit volume, \(\rho = m/V\), and is an intensive property.
  • Pressure is force per unit area, \(P = F/A\), and increases with depth as \(P = P_0 + \rho gh\).
  • Archimedes' principle gives the buoyant force as the weight of displaced fluid, \(F_b = \rho_{\text{fluid}}V_{\text{disp}}g\).
  • For steady, incompressible flow, continuity requires \(A_1v_1 = A_2v_2\).
  • Bernoulli's equation expresses energy conservation per unit volume for an ideal fluid.
  • Where a horizontal pipe narrows, the fluid speeds up and the pressure falls.
  • Stress, strain, and the elastic moduli describe how bulk materials deform under forces.
