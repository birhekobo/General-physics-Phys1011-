Introduction to Physics

Welcome to Physics!

Physics is the fundamental science that seeks to understand the behavior of the universe at all scales, from the smallest subatomic particles to the largest galaxies. Through observation, experimentation, and mathematical modeling, physics provides us with the principles to explain natural phenomena and develop technologies that shape our modern world.

Physics is also an experimental science. A claim becomes physics only when it can be tested against measurement. The cycle is familiar: observe a phenomenon, propose a model, predict a measurable consequence, perform the experiment, and revise the model when the data disagree. Mathematics is the language in which those predictions are written, which is why this chapter begins with measurement and vectors rather than with motion.

This chapter lays the groundwork for your study of physics by introducing the essential tools and concepts you'll need: measurement systems, uncertainty analysis, and vector mathematics. These fundamentals will provide the foundation for all subsequent topics in this course.

1.1 Physical Quantities and Units of Measurement

The foundation of physics and engineering is the measurement of physical quantities. To communicate these measurements effectively and unambiguously, a standardized system of units is essential. A number without a unit is incomplete: saying that a table is "2 long" says nothing until you add metres, feet, or cubits.

1.1.1 Quantities and Units

Definition: Physical Quantity and Unit

A physical quantity is a property of a phenomenon, body, or substance that can be quantified by measurement. Examples include length, mass, time, electric current, and force.

A unit is a particular physical quantity, defined and adopted by convention, with which other quantities of the same kind are compared to express their value.

Measurement is the process of assigning a number to a physical quantity by comparing it with a unit. Every measurement therefore has two parts: a numerical value and a unit.

For example:

  • Physical quantity: length
  • Numerical value: 5.2
  • Unit: metre (m)
  • Expression: \(5.2\,\text{m}\)

The same length can be written as \(5.2\,\text{m}\), \(520\,\text{cm}\), or \(0.0052\,\text{km}\). The physical quantity is unchanged; only the unit and the numerical value change together. This is the practical meaning of a conversion factor: it is a ratio equal to one, so multiplying by it rescales the number without changing the quantity.

Key Concept: Units Behave Like Algebraic Symbols

Units can be multiplied, divided, and cancelled exactly as algebraic symbols are. In the expression \(60\,\text{km/h}\), the slash means "per", so the unit is \(\text{km}\cdot\text{h}^{-1}\). Treating units this way makes unit conversion and dimensional checking almost automatic.

The International System of Units (SI, from the French Système International d'Unités) is built on seven base quantities. Every other unit is derived from these seven.

Example: Expressing Derived Units

The unit of force is the newton (N). Show how it is built from base units, and do the same for the joule (J).

Solution

From Newton's second law, \(F = ma\). The unit of mass is the kilogram and the unit of acceleration is metres per second squared, so:

\(1\,\text{N} = 1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2 = 1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}\)

Work is force times distance, so the joule is a newton-metre:

\(1\,\text{J} = 1\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m} = 1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s}^{-2}\)

These expansions are useful when checking whether an equation is dimensionally consistent.

Key Concept: Coherent SI Units

In SI, derived units are coherent: no numerical factor appears when they are combined. One newton is exactly one kilogram-metre per second squared, with no conversion constant. This is why SI calculations can be done directly, provided every input is first converted to base units.

1.1.3 Conversion of Units

Converting between units is an essential skill in physics. The key is to multiply by conversion factors that equal 1. Because multiplying by 1 does not change a quantity, the method is safe as long as each factor is written with the unwanted unit in the denominator so that it cancels.

Example: Converting a Speed

Convert a speed of \(60\,\text{miles per hour}\) to metres per second.

Solution

We know that:

\(1\,\text{mile} = 1609\,\text{m}, \qquad 1\,\text{hour} = 3600\,\text{s}\)

Write each relation as a fraction equal to one and arrange the units to cancel:

\(60\,\frac{\text{mi}}{\text{h}} = 60\,\frac{\text{mi}}{\text{h}} \times \frac{1609\,\text{m}}{1\,\text{mi}} \times \frac{1\,\text{h}}{3600\,\text{s}}\)
\(= 60 \times \frac{1609}{3600}\,\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \approx 26.8\,\text{m/s}\)

The mile and hour units cancel, leaving metres per second. A useful check is that \(60\,\text{mph}\) is roughly \(27\,\text{m/s}\), close to the familiar value of \(100\,\text{km/h} \approx 28\,\text{m/s}\).

Practice Problem: Conversion Chain

Convert \(90\,\text{km/h}\) to metres per second, and convert a density of \(2.70\,\text{g/cm}^3\) to \(\text{kg/m}^3\).

Solution

For the speed:

\(90\,\frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}} \times \frac{1000\,\text{m}}{1\,\text{km}} \times \frac{1\,\text{h}}{3600\,\text{s}} = \frac{90\,000}{3600}\,\text{m/s} = 25\,\text{m/s}\)

For the density, both the mass unit and the length unit must be converted, and the length appears cubed:

\(2.70\,\frac{\text{g}}{\text{cm}^3} \times \frac{1\,\text{kg}}{1000\,\text{g}} \times \left(\frac{100\,\text{cm}}{1\,\text{m}}\right)^3 = 2.70 \times \frac{10^6}{10^3}\,\text{kg/m}^3 = 2.70 \times 10^3\,\text{kg/m}^3\)

The factor is \(10^3\), not \(10^2\), because the centimetre-to-metre conversion is cubed.

1.1.4 Dimensional Analysis

Every physical quantity carries a dimension, which describes the kind of quantity it is independently of the units used. Length has dimension \([L]\), mass has dimension \([M]\), and time has dimension \([T]\). Derived quantities have dimensions built from these base dimensions. For example, speed has dimensions:

\([v] = \frac{[L]}{[T]} = LT^{-1}\)

while force has dimensions:

\([F] = [M][L][T]^{-2} = MLT^{-2}\)

Dimensional analysis cannot supply numerical factors, but it can test whether an equation is plausible, convert units, and predict how a quantity scales when the inputs change. The rule is simple: every term added or equated must have the same dimensions.

Example: Checking an Equation

Show that the kinematic equation \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2ax\) is dimensionally consistent.

Solution

The left side has dimensions \((LT^{-1})^2 = L^2T^{-2}\). The first term on the right, \(v_0^2\), has the same dimensions. The second term is:

\([a][x] = (LT^{-2})(L) = L^2T^{-2}\)

All three terms share the dimensions \(L^2T^{-2}\), so the equation is dimensionally consistent. This check would immediately reveal an incorrect expression such as \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a/x\), whose second term has dimensions \(LT^{-3}\).

Key Concept: What Dimensional Analysis Cannot Do

A dimensionally correct equation may still be wrong by a dimensionless factor, such as \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) or \(2\pi\). Dimensional analysis is a consistency check, not a proof. It also cannot distinguish between quantities with the same dimensions: torque and energy both have dimensions \(ML^2T^{-2}\), yet they mean different things.

Example: Testing a Pendulum Formula

The period of a simple pendulum is suspected to be \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\), where \(l\) is the length and \(g\) is the acceleration due to gravity. Check the dimensions.

Solution

The quantity inside the square root has dimensions:

\(\left[\frac{l}{g}\right] = \frac{L}{LT^{-2}} = T^2\)

Taking the square root gives \(T\), which matches the dimension of a period. The factor \(2\pi\) is dimensionless and cannot be found by dimensional analysis, but the structure of the formula is at least plausible.

1.1.5 Order-of-Magnitude Estimates

Physicists often need a quick answer before doing a precise calculation. An order-of-magnitude estimate (sometimes called a Fermi estimate) rounds every input to one significant figure and aims only for the nearest power of ten. The purpose is to decide whether a detailed calculation is worth doing and whether its result is reasonable.

Definition: Order of Magnitude

The order of magnitude of a quantity is the nearest power of ten to its value. Two quantities differ by one order of magnitude when one is about ten times larger than the other. A result quoted as "about \(10^3\)" is an order-of-magnitude estimate.

Example: Mass of Air in a Room

Estimate the mass of air in a room measuring \(5\,\text{m} \times 4\,\text{m} \times 3\,\text{m}\). The density of air is about \(1.2\,\text{kg/m}^3\).

Solution

The volume is:

\(V = (5)(4)(3) = 60\,\text{m}^3\)

The mass is density times volume:

\(m = \rho V \approx (1.2)(60) \approx 72\,\text{kg}\)

So the air in a small room has a mass of roughly \(70\,\text{kg}\), comparable to the mass of an adult. The estimate is surprising to many people precisely because air is invisible, which is why such calculations are valuable.

Practice Problem: A Fermi Estimate

Estimate the number of times a person breathes in one day. State each assumption you make.

Solution

Assume a resting breathing rate of about 15 breaths per minute. Then:

\(15\,\frac{\text{breaths}}{\text{min}} \times 60\,\frac{\text{min}}{\text{h}} \times 24\,\frac{\text{h}}{\text{day}} \approx 2.2 \times 10^4\,\text{breaths/day}\)

The answer is about \(2 \times 10^4\), or twenty thousand breaths per day. Different assumptions about activity level change the prefactor but not the order of magnitude.

Key Concept: Estimation Is a Skill

Good estimates are built from quantities you already know: your height, the speed of a car, the population of a city, the density of water. Breaking an unfamiliar quantity into familiar factors is more reliable than guessing a single number, and it makes the assumptions visible so that they can be checked.

1.2 Uncertainty in Measurement and Significant Figures

All measurements are approximations. No instrument is perfect, no observer is perfectly consistent, and every physical quantity has some spread in its value. A useful measurement therefore reports not only the best estimate but also how much that estimate might be off. This section explains how uncertainty is expressed, how it propagates through calculations, and how significant figures communicate precision without overstating it.

1.2.1 Uncertainty, Accuracy, and Precision

Definition: Uncertainty and Error

The uncertainty of a measurement is a quantitative estimate of the range within which the true value is likely to lie. It is often denoted by \(\delta\) (delta) and has the same unit as the quantity being measured.

The absolute uncertainty is stated in the units of the measurement, as in \(5.2 \pm 0.1\,\text{cm}\). The relative uncertainty is the ratio \(\delta x / x\), and the percentage uncertainty is that ratio multiplied by \(100\%\).

Error is the difference between a measured value and the true value. Unlike uncertainty, the true error is usually unknown; uncertainty is our honest estimate of its possible size.

A measurement is often written as:

\(\text{measured value} = \text{best estimate} \pm \text{uncertainty}\)

For example, a length might be reported as \(l = 5.2\,\text{cm} \pm 0.1\,\text{cm}\). The relative uncertainty is:

\(\frac{\delta l}{l} = \frac{0.1}{5.2} \approx 0.019 = 1.9\%\)

The same absolute uncertainty of \(0.1\,\text{cm}\) would represent a much larger relative uncertainty for a length of \(0.5\,\text{cm}\). This is why relative uncertainty is the better measure of measurement quality.

Two words are often confused when describing measurements. Accuracy describes how close a measured value is to the true value, while precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree with one another. A digital balance that always reads \(0.50\,\text{g}\) too high is precise but inaccurate; a ruler read carelessly may be accurate on average but imprecise. Good experimental design aims for both.

Figure 1.2: Accuracy is about the centre of the pattern; precision is about the tightness of the pattern. A measurement system can fail in either respect independently.
Definition: Random and Systematic Error

Random error varies unpredictably from one measurement to the next. It tends to average out when many readings are combined, and it limits precision.

Systematic error shifts every measurement in the same direction, for example because an instrument is zeroed incorrectly or a ruler has been stretched. Repeating the measurement does not remove it, and it limits accuracy.

Key Concept: Uncertainty Is Not a Mistake

Reporting an uncertainty does not mean the experiment failed. It means the experimenter understands the limits of the apparatus and the procedure. A result of \(9.81 \pm 0.02\,\text{m/s}^2\) is more informative than a bare \(9.81\,\text{m/s}^2\), because it tells the reader how seriously to take the last digits.

1.2.2 Propagating Uncertainty

Uncertainty also propagates through calculations. The rules below give a conservative estimate of the final uncertainty, suitable for introductory laboratory work.

Sums and differences. Absolute uncertainties add:

\(\delta(A \pm B) \approx \delta A + \delta B\)

Products and quotients. Fractional uncertainties add:

\(\frac{\delta(AB)}{AB} \approx \frac{\delta A}{A} + \frac{\delta B}{B}, \qquad \frac{\delta(A/B)}{A/B} \approx \frac{\delta A}{A} + \frac{\delta B}{B}\)

Powers. A power multiplies the fractional uncertainty by the exponent:

\(\frac{\delta(A^n)}{A^n} \approx |n|\frac{\delta A}{A}\)

These rules explain why a chain of measurements rarely becomes more precise than its least precise link. They also show why squaring a quantity doubles its fractional uncertainty.

Example: Area of a Rectangle

The sides of a rectangle are measured as \(a = (8.0 \pm 0.1)\,\text{cm}\) and \(b = (5.0 \pm 0.1)\,\text{cm}\). Estimate the area and its uncertainty.

Solution

The area is \(A = ab = (8.0)(5.0) = 40.0\,\text{cm}^2\). The fractional uncertainties are \(0.1/8.0 = 0.0125\) and \(0.1/5.0 = 0.020\). Adding them gives a fractional uncertainty of about \(0.0325\), so:

\(\delta A \approx 0.0325 \times 40.0 \approx 1.3\,\text{cm}^2\)

Thus \(A = (40.0 \pm 1.3)\,\text{cm}^2\), which should be reported as \((40 \pm 1)\,\text{cm}^2\) to match the precision of the data. Quoting \(40.0 \pm 1.3\) would overstate what the measurements can support.

Practice Problem: Uncertainty in a Speed

A car travels \(d = (120 \pm 2)\,\text{m}\) in a time \(t = (6.0 \pm 0.1)\,\text{s}\). Estimate the speed and its absolute uncertainty.

Solution

The speed is:

\(v = \frac{d}{t} = \frac{120}{6.0} = 20\,\text{m/s}\)

The fractional uncertainties are \(2/120 = 0.0167\) and \(0.1/6.0 = 0.0167\). Their sum is about \(0.033\), so:

\(\delta v \approx 0.033 \times 20 \approx 0.7\,\text{m/s}\)

The result is \(v = (20 \pm 0.7)\,\text{m/s}\), or about \((20 \pm 1)\,\text{m/s}\).

1.2.3 Significant Figures

Significant figures indicate the precision of a measurement. They are the digits that carry real information about the quantity, including the last digit, which is estimated. Writing too many digits suggests a precision that the instrument does not have; writing too few throws away information.

Key Concept: Rules for Significant Figures
  1. Non-zero digits are always significant.
  2. Zeros between non-zero digits are significant.
  3. Leading zeros are not significant; they only locate the decimal point.
  4. Trailing zeros are significant if there is a decimal point.
  5. In scientific notation, all digits before the power of ten are significant.

For example, \(0.00450\,\text{m}\) has three significant figures: the leading zeros merely place the decimal point, while the trailing zero after the 5 is significant because it follows a decimal point. The number \(1200\,\text{kg}\) is ambiguous: it may have two, three, or four significant figures. Writing \(1.2 \times 10^3\,\text{kg}\) removes the ambiguity and states that there are two significant figures.

Practice Problem: Counting Significant Figures

How many significant digits are in the following measurements?

  1. \(0.00340\,\text{m}\)
  2. \(1200\,\text{kg}\)
  3. \(1.20 \times 10^3\,\text{kg}\)
  4. \(450.00\,\text{s}\)
Solution
  1. \(0.00340\,\text{m}\) has 3 significant digits (3, 4, 0).
  2. \(1200\,\text{kg}\) has 2 significant digits (1, 2) under the usual convention, but the notation is ambiguous.
  3. \(1.20 \times 10^3\,\text{kg}\) has 3 significant digits (1, 2, 0).
  4. \(450.00\,\text{s}\) has 5 significant digits (4, 5, 0, 0, 0).

1.2.4 Significant Figures in Calculations

Two different rules apply, depending on the operation.

  • Multiplication and division: the result carries the same number of significant figures as the input with the fewest significant figures.
  • Addition and subtraction: the result carries the same number of decimal places as the input with the fewest decimal places.
Example: Rounding a Calculation

Evaluate \(2.5 \times 3.42\) and \(12.11 + 18.0 + 1.013\), rounding each result correctly.

Solution

For the product, \(2.5 \times 3.42 = 8.55\). The factor \(2.5\) has two significant figures, so the result must have two:

\(2.5 \times 3.42 \approx 8.6\)

For the sum, the unrounded result is \(31.123\). The term \(18.0\) has one decimal place, so the answer must have one decimal place:

\(12.11 + 18.0 + 1.013 \approx 31.1\)

Notice that the addition rule is about decimal places, not significant figures: \(18.0\) has three significant figures but only one decimal place.

Key Concept: Keep Guard Digits

Do not round at every intermediate step. Carry one or two extra digits through the calculation, then round only the final answer. Rounding early can change the last digit of the result, especially in multi-step problems.

1.2.5 Scientific Notation and Unit Prefixes

Definition: Scientific Notation

A quantity is in scientific notation when it is written as \(a \times 10^n\), where \(1 \le |a| < 10\) and \(n\) is an integer. The exponent records the order of magnitude, and the digits of \(a\) record the significant figures.

Scientific notation is convenient for very large and very small quantities. For example:

\(0.00042 = 4.2 \times 10^{-4}, \qquad 6\,300\,000 = 6.3 \times 10^{6}\)

It also removes the ambiguity of trailing zeros, because every digit written in the coefficient is significant.

1.3 Vectors: Composition and Resolution

Many physical quantities are not fully described by a number and a unit. A displacement of \(5\,\text{km}\) is incomplete until we know in which direction; a force of \(20\,\text{N}\) is incomplete until we know along which line it acts. Such quantities are vectors, and the mathematics of vectors is the language of mechanics, electromagnetism, and geometry.

1.3.1 Scalars and Vectors

Definition: Scalar and Vector

A scalar is a physical quantity specified completely by a magnitude and a unit. Mass, time, temperature, speed, and energy are scalars.

A vector is a physical quantity specified by both a magnitude and a direction. Displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, and electric field are vectors.

The magnitude of a vector is its length, a non-negative scalar written \(|\vec{A}|\) or simply \(A\). The direction may be given by an angle, by a unit vector, or by components.

Vectors are written with an arrow, as in \(\vec{A}\), or in bold type, as in **A**. Two vectors are equal only if they have the same magnitude and the same direction; their starting points do not matter. This freedom to translate a vector is what makes the head-to-tail construction of vector addition possible.

Key Concept: Notation and Magnitude

The magnitude \(A = |\vec{A}|\) is always non-negative. The negative of a vector, \(-\vec{A}\), has the same magnitude but the opposite direction. Do not confuse the scalar \(-2\) with a vector: \(-\vec{A}\) is a reversal in space, not a negative length.

1.3.2 Vector Addition

Vectors are added by placing them head to tail. If \(\vec{A}\) is drawn first and \(\vec{B}\) is drawn starting from the head of \(\vec{A}\), then the resultant \(\vec{R} = \vec{A} + \vec{B}\) is the arrow from the tail of \(\vec{A}\) to the head of \(\vec{B}\). This is the triangle law of vector addition.

Drawing \(\vec{A}\) and \(\vec{B}\) from a common origin and completing the parallelogram gives the same resultant along the diagonal. This is the parallelogram law. Because the order of the two vectors does not matter, vector addition is commutative:

\(\vec{A} + \vec{B} = \vec{B} + \vec{A}\)

It is also associative: \((\vec{A} + \vec{B}) + \vec{C} = \vec{A} + (\vec{B} + \vec{C})\).

Figure 1.4: Two equivalent constructions for \(\vec{R} = \vec{A} + \vec{B}\). The dashed sides of the parallelogram are parallel to the original vectors.

The geometric construction is excellent for intuition, but calculations are usually easier in components. Since perpendicular components are independent, addition is performed term by term:

\(\vec{R} = \vec{A} + \vec{B} = (A_x + B_x)\hat{i} + (A_y + B_y)\hat{j} + (A_z + B_z)\hat{k}\)

The magnitude of the resultant then follows from the Pythagorean relation, and its direction from an inverse tangent.

Figure 1.5: A hiker walks \(3.0\,\text{km}\) east and then \(4.0\,\text{km}\) north. The resultant displacement has magnitude \(5.0\,\text{km}\) and points \(53^\circ\) north of east.
Example: Resultant Displacement

A hiker walks \(3.0\,\text{km}\) due east, then \(4.0\,\text{km}\) due north. Find the magnitude and direction of the total displacement.

Solution

Take east as \(+x\) and north as \(+y\). The two displacements are:

\(\vec{d}_1 = 3.0\hat{i}\,\text{km}, \qquad \vec{d}_2 = 4.0\hat{j}\,\text{km}\)

The resultant is \(\vec{R} = 3.0\hat{i} + 4.0\hat{j}\,\text{km}\). Its magnitude is:

\(R = \sqrt{3.0^2 + 4.0^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5.0\,\text{km}\)

The angle measured from east toward north is:

\(\theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{4.0}{3.0}\right) \approx 53^\circ\)

The displacement is \(5.0\,\text{km}\) at \(53^\circ\) north of east. Notice that the distance travelled is \(7.0\,\text{km}\), which is larger than the magnitude of the displacement; distance and displacement are different quantities.

Key Concept: When Does \(|\vec{A} + \vec{B}| = A + B\)?

The magnitude of a vector sum equals the sum of the magnitudes only when the vectors point in the same direction. If they are perpendicular, \(|\vec{A} + \vec{B}| = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}\). If they point in opposite directions, the magnitude is \(|A - B|\). In general, the triangle inequality gives \(\big||\vec{A}| - |\vec{B}|\big| \le |\vec{A} + \vec{B}| \le |\vec{A}| + |\vec{B}|\).

1.3.3 Vector Subtraction and Scalar Multiplication

Subtraction is addition of the opposite vector:

\(\vec{A} - \vec{B} = \vec{A} + (-\vec{B})\)

Geometrically, \(-\vec{B}\) has the same length as \(\vec{B}\) but points in the opposite direction. Equivalently, \(\vec{A} - \vec{B}\) is the vector drawn from the head of \(\vec{B}\) to the head of \(\vec{A}\) when both start from the same point. In components, subtraction is performed term by term:

\(\vec{A} - \vec{B} = (A_x - B_x)\hat{i} + (A_y - B_y)\hat{j} + (A_z - B_z)\hat{k}\)

Multiplying a vector by a scalar \(k\) changes its length by the factor \(|k|\); if \(k < 0\), the direction reverses:

\(k\vec{A} = (kA_x)\hat{i} + (kA_y)\hat{j} + (kA_z)\hat{k}\)

Scalar multiplication obeys the usual distributive laws, \(k(\vec{A} + \vec{B}) = k\vec{A} + k\vec{B}\) and \((k + m)\vec{A} = k\vec{A} + m\vec{A}\).

Figure 1.6: Left: \(\vec{A} - \vec{B}\) joins the head of \(\vec{B}\) to the head of \(\vec{A}\). Right: multiplying by a scalar stretches a vector; a negative scalar also reverses it.

The relative vector from an object at position \(\vec{r}_1\) to an object at \(\vec{r}_2\) is \(\vec{r}_{12} = \vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1\). This construction is used throughout kinematics to describe displacements and relative motion. If the two objects move with velocities \(\vec{v}_1\) and \(\vec{v}_2\), the velocity of the second relative to the first is \(\vec{v}_{21} = \vec{v}_2 - \vec{v}_1\).

Example: Vector Subtraction

Given \(\vec{A} = 5\hat{i} + 2\hat{j}\) and \(\vec{B} = 2\hat{i} + 6\hat{j}\), find \(\vec{A} - \vec{B}\) and its magnitude.

Solution
\(\vec{A} - \vec{B} = (5 - 2)\hat{i} + (2 - 6)\hat{j} = 3\hat{i} - 4\hat{j}\)

The magnitude is:

\(|\vec{A} - \vec{B}| = \sqrt{3^2 + (-4)^2} = 5\)

The result points into the fourth quadrant, \(4\) units down for every \(3\) units right.

1.3.4 Components of a Vector

Any vector can be resolved into components along coordinate axes. In two dimensions, with \(\theta\) measured counterclockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis:

\(A_x = A\cos\theta, \qquad A_y = A\sin\theta\)

In three dimensions the vector is written:

\(\vec{A} = A_x\hat{i} + A_y\hat{j} + A_z\hat{k}\)

Given the components, the magnitude and direction follow from:

\(A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2 + A_z^2}, \qquad \theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{A_y}{A_x}\right)\)

where \(\theta\) is measured from the positive \(x\)-axis. The inverse tangent must be placed in the correct quadrant: for example, a vector with \(A_x < 0\) and \(A_y > 0\) lies in the second quadrant, so \(180^\circ\) must be added to the calculator result. In three dimensions the direction is specified by the direction cosines:

\(\cos\alpha = \frac{A_x}{A}, \qquad \cos\beta = \frac{A_y}{A}, \qquad \cos\gamma = \frac{A_z}{A}\)

which satisfy \(\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1\).

Figure 1.7: Components are signed. The angle \(\theta\) is always measured counterclockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis, so in the second quadrant \(\theta\) is between \(90^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\).
Practice Problem: Components from Magnitude and Angle

A vector \(\vec{A}\) has a magnitude of \(10\) units and makes an angle of \(30^\circ\) with the positive \(x\)-axis. Find its \(x\) and \(y\) components.

Solution
\(A_x = A \cos\theta = 10 \cos 30^\circ = 10 \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = 5\sqrt{3} \approx 8.66\ \text{units}\)
\(A_y = A \sin\theta = 10 \sin 30^\circ = 10 \times \frac{1}{2} = 5\ \text{units}\)

Check: \(\sqrt{8.66^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{75 + 25} = 10\), as required.

Key Concept: Choosing Axes

Coordinate axes are a convenience, not part of the physics. Choose them so that as many vectors as possible lie along an axis; this reduces the number of components you must resolve. The magnitude and direction of the resultant do not depend on the choice of axes.

1.4 Unit Vectors

Unit vectors have a magnitude of 1 and are used to specify direction without carrying any scale information. The standard basis vectors \(\hat{i}\), \(\hat{j}\), and \(\hat{k}\) point along the positive \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) axes respectively, and they are mutually perpendicular.

1.4.1 Finding a Unit Vector

The unit vector in the direction of vector \(\vec{A}\) is given by:

\(\hat{A} = \frac{\vec{A}}{|\vec{A}|}\)

Dividing by the magnitude preserves the direction and rescales the length to 1. Conversely, any vector can be reconstructed from its magnitude and direction: \(\vec{A} = A\hat{A}\).

Figure 1.8: The unit vector \(\hat{A} = \vec{A}/|\vec{A}|\) retains the direction of \(\vec{A}\) and has magnitude 1.
Example: Unit Vector in Three Dimensions

Find the unit vector in the direction of \(\vec{A} = 3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j} - 5\hat{k}\).

Solution

First, find the magnitude of \(\vec{A}\):

\(|\vec{A}| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2 + (-5)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16 + 25} = \sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt{2}\)

The unit vector is:

\(\hat{A} = \frac{3}{5\sqrt{2}}\hat{i} + \frac{4}{5\sqrt{2}}\hat{j} - \frac{5}{5\sqrt{2}}\hat{k} \approx 0.424\hat{i} + 0.566\hat{j} - 0.707\hat{k}\)

As a check, the squares of the coefficients sum to \(0.424^2 + 0.566^2 + 0.707^2 \approx 1\).

Practice Problem: A Unit Vector

Find the unit vector in the direction of \(\vec{B} = -2\hat{i} + \hat{j} + 2\hat{k}\).

Solution

The magnitude is:

\(|\vec{B}| = \sqrt{(-2)^2 + 1^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 4} = 3\)

Therefore:

\(\hat{B} = -\frac{2}{3}\hat{i} + \frac{1}{3}\hat{j} + \frac{2}{3}\hat{k}\)
Key Concept: Unit Vectors Carry Direction Only

A unit vector has no units and no physical size; it is a pure direction. The notation \(\hat{A}\) therefore says "the direction of \(\vec{A}\)", while \(A\hat{A}\) says "magnitude \(A\) in that direction". This separation is used constantly in physics, from the definition of the electric field to the unit tangent vector of a curve.

1.5 Dot Product (Scalar Product)

The dot product of two vectors is a scalar quantity that results from multiplying the magnitudes of the vectors and the cosine of the angle between them. Geometrically, it measures how much one vector points along another; physically, it is the operation behind work, power, and flux.

1.5.1 Definition and Formula

Definition: Dot Product

The dot product (or scalar product) of two vectors \(\vec{A}\) and \(\vec{B}\) is the scalar

\(\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = |\vec{A}||\vec{B}|\cos\theta\)

where \(\theta\) is the angle between the vectors, taken in the range \(0^\circ \le \theta \le 180^\circ\).

In component form, if \(\vec{A} = A_x\hat{i} + A_y\hat{j} + A_z\hat{k}\) and \(\vec{B} = B_x\hat{i} + B_y\hat{j} + B_z\hat{k}\), then:

\(\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z\)

The two forms are equivalent. The component form is usually faster for calculation; the geometric form reveals meaning.

Figure 1.9: The dot product is the projection of one vector onto the other, multiplied by the length of the second: \(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B} = (A\cos\theta)B\).

The sign of the dot product tells you the angle:

  • If \(\theta < 90^\circ\), then \(\cos\theta > 0\) and the dot product is positive; the vectors point generally in the same direction.
  • If \(\theta = 90^\circ\), then \(\cos\theta = 0\) and the dot product is zero; the vectors are perpendicular.
  • If \(\theta > 90^\circ\), then \(\cos\theta < 0\) and the dot product is negative; the vectors point generally in opposite directions.
Key Concept: A Dot Product of Zero

If \(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B} = 0\) and neither vector is the zero vector, then the vectors must be perpendicular. This is one of the fastest tests for orthogonality in physics and engineering. It does not imply that either vector is zero.

1.5.2 Properties of the Dot Product

  1. Commutative: \(\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = \vec{B} \cdot \vec{A}\)
  2. Distributive: \(\vec{A} \cdot (\vec{B} + \vec{C}) = \vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} + \vec{A} \cdot \vec{C}\)
  3. Scalar multiplication: \((k\vec{A}) \cdot \vec{B} = k(\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B})\)
  4. Self-product: \(\vec{A} \cdot \vec{A} = |\vec{A}|^2 = A^2\)
  5. Orthogonality: if \(\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = 0\) and neither vector is zero, the vectors are perpendicular

For the basis vectors, \(\hat{i}\cdot\hat{i} = \hat{j}\cdot\hat{j} = \hat{k}\cdot\hat{k} = 1\), while \(\hat{i}\cdot\hat{j} = \hat{j}\cdot\hat{k} = \hat{k}\cdot\hat{i} = 0\). These relations generate the component formula.

Example: Computing a Dot Product

Find the dot product of vectors \(\vec{A} = 2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} - \hat{k}\) and \(\vec{B} = -\hat{i} + 4\hat{j} + 2\hat{k}\).

Solution

Using the component formula:

\(\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = (2)(-1) + (3)(4) + (-1)(2) = -2 + 12 - 2 = 8\)

The result is positive, so the angle between the vectors is acute. The dot product is a scalar: it has no direction, although its sign carries geometric information.

1.5.3 The Angle Between Two Vectors

Rearranging the geometric definition gives the angle between two vectors:

\(\cos\theta = \frac{\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B}}{|\vec{A}||\vec{B}|}, \qquad \theta = \arccos\!\left(\frac{\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B}}{|\vec{A}||\vec{B}|}\right)\)
Practice Problem: Angle Between Vectors

Given vectors \(\vec{P} = 3\hat{i} - 2\hat{j} + 4\hat{k}\) and \(\vec{Q} = \hat{i} + 5\hat{j} - 2\hat{k}\), calculate:

  1. \(\vec{P} \cdot \vec{Q}\)
  2. The angle between \(\vec{P}\) and \(\vec{Q}\)
Solution
  1. \(\vec{P} \cdot \vec{Q} = (3)(1) + (-2)(5) + (4)(-2) = 3 - 10 - 8 = -15\)
  2. First find magnitudes:
    \(|\vec{P}| = \sqrt{3^2 + (-2)^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 4 + 16} = \sqrt{29}\)
    \(|\vec{Q}| = \sqrt{1^2 + 5^2 + (-2)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 25 + 4} = \sqrt{30}\)

    Then:

    \(\cos\theta = \frac{\vec{P} \cdot \vec{Q}}{|\vec{P}||\vec{Q}|} = \frac{-15}{\sqrt{29}\sqrt{30}} \approx \frac{-15}{29.5} \approx -0.508\)
    \(\theta = \arccos(-0.508) \approx 120.5^\circ\)

    The obtuse angle is consistent with the negative dot product.

1.5.4 Vector Projection

The dot product measures how much one vector points along another. The scalar projection of \(\vec{A}\) onto \(\vec{B}\) is:

\(A_{\parallel} = |\vec{A}|\cos\theta = \frac{\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}}{|\vec{B}|}\)

and the vector projection is:

\(\vec{A}_{\parallel} = \left(\frac{\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}}{|\vec{B}|^2}\right)\vec{B} = (A_{\parallel})\hat{B}\)

The component of \(\vec{A}\) perpendicular to \(\vec{B}\) is then \(\vec{A}_{\perp} = \vec{A} - \vec{A}_{\parallel}\), and the two pieces satisfy \(\vec{A} = \vec{A}_{\parallel} + \vec{A}_{\perp}\).

Example: Force Along a Displacement

Find the component of \(\vec{F} = 6\hat{i} + 8\hat{j}\,\text{N}\) along the direction of \(\vec{d} = 3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j}\,\text{m}\).

Solution

The magnitude of \(\vec{d}\) is \(\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5\,\text{m}\), so the unit vector along \(\vec{d}\) is \(\hat{d} = (3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j})/5\). Therefore:

\(F_{\parallel} = \vec{F}\cdot\hat{d} = \frac{(6)(3) + (8)(4)}{5} = \frac{50}{5} = 10\,\text{N}\)

Only this component does work when the object moves along \(\vec{d}\). The work is \(W = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{d} = 50\,\text{J}\), the same as \(F_{\parallel}|\vec{d}| = (10)(5)\).

1.5.5 Applications of the Dot Product

  1. Finding the angle between two vectors.
  2. Projecting one vector onto another.
  3. Computing work done by a force: \(W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d}\).
  4. Testing whether two vectors are perpendicular.
  5. Computing power: \(P = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{v}\).

In physics, the work done by a constant force \(\vec{F}\) acting on an object that undergoes a displacement \(\vec{d}\) is given by the dot product \(W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d}\). This means only the component of force in the direction of displacement contributes to the work. A force perpendicular to the motion does no work at all, which is why the magnetic force on a moving charge changes its direction but not its speed.

Example: Work at an Angle

A force of \(50\,\text{N}\) pulls a crate at \(30^\circ\) above the horizontal through a displacement of \(10\,\text{m}\) along the floor. How much work does the force do?

Solution
\(W = Fd\cos\theta = (50)(10)\cos 30^\circ = 500 \times 0.866 \approx 433\,\text{J}\)

The vertical component of the force does no work because it is perpendicular to the displacement. Only the horizontal component, \(F\cos\theta = 43.3\,\text{N}\), contributes.

1.6 Cross Product (Vector Product)

The cross product of two vectors is a vector that is perpendicular to both original vectors, with a magnitude equal to the area of the parallelogram formed by the two vectors. It is the operation behind torque, angular momentum, magnetic force, and the geometry of surfaces.

1.6.1 Definition and the Right-Hand Rule

Definition: Cross Product

The cross product (or vector product) of two vectors \(\vec{A}\) and \(\vec{B}\) is the vector

\(\vec{A} \times \vec{B} = |\vec{A}||\vec{B}|\sin\theta\,\hat{n}\)

where \(\theta\) is the angle between the vectors and \(\hat{n}\) is a unit vector perpendicular to both \(\vec{A}\) and \(\vec{B}\), with its sense fixed by the right-hand rule.

The right-hand rule fixes the direction of \(\hat{n}\): point the fingers of your right hand along \(\vec{A}\) and curl them toward \(\vec{B}\) through the smaller angle; your extended thumb points along \(\vec{A}\times\vec{B}\). Reversing the order of the vectors reverses the thumb, which is why the cross product is anticommutative. The magnitude \(|\vec{A}||\vec{B}|\sin\theta\) is largest when the vectors are perpendicular and zero when they are parallel.

Figure 1.10: Left: the right-hand rule gives the direction of \(\vec{A}\times\vec{B}\). Right: the magnitude \(AB\sin\theta\) is the area of the parallelogram spanned by the vectors.

1.6.2 Component Form and the Determinant

In component form:

\(\vec{A} \times \vec{B} = (A_yB_z - A_zB_y)\hat{i} + (A_zB_x - A_xB_z)\hat{j} + (A_xB_y - A_yB_x)\hat{k}\)

This is often remembered using the determinant formula:

\(\vec{A} \times \vec{B} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ A_x & A_y & A_z \\ B_x & B_y & B_z \end{vmatrix}\)

Expanding along the first row gives the component expression above. The determinant is only a memory aid: the entries include vectors, so it is evaluated by the usual cofactor rule with the basis vectors carried along.

Example: Computing a Cross Product

Find the cross product of vectors \(\vec{A} = 2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} - \hat{k}\) and \(\vec{B} = -\hat{i} + 4\hat{j} + 2\hat{k}\).

Solution

Using the determinant formula:

\(\vec{A} \times \vec{B} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 2 & 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 4 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = \hat{i}(3 \cdot 2 - (-1) \cdot 4) - \hat{j}(2 \cdot 2 - (-1) \cdot (-1)) + \hat{k}(2 \cdot 4 - 3 \cdot (-1))\)
\(= \hat{i}(6 + 4) - \hat{j}(4 - 1) + \hat{k}(8 + 3) = 10\hat{i} - 3\hat{j} + 11\hat{k}\)

Check that the result is perpendicular to both inputs by taking dot products:

\((10\hat{i} - 3\hat{j} + 11\hat{k})\cdot(2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} - \hat{k}) = 20 - 9 - 11 = 0\)

The dot product with \(\vec{A}\) is zero, and a similar check with \(\vec{B}\) also gives zero, as it must.

1.6.3 Properties of the Cross Product

  1. Anti-commutative: \(\vec{A} \times \vec{B} = -(\vec{B} \times \vec{A})\)
  2. Distributive: \(\vec{A} \times (\vec{B} + \vec{C}) = \vec{A} \times \vec{B} + \vec{A} \times \vec{C}\)
  3. Scalar multiplication: \((k\vec{A}) \times \vec{B} = k(\vec{A} \times \vec{B})\)
  4. Parallel vectors: if \(\vec{A} \times \vec{B} = \vec{0}\) and neither vector is zero, the vectors are parallel
  5. Magnitude equals the area of the parallelogram formed by the vectors
  6. Not associative in general: \((\vec{A} \times \vec{B}) \times \vec{C} \neq \vec{A} \times (\vec{B} \times \vec{C})\)

For the basis vectors, the cyclic relations are \(\hat{i}\times\hat{j} = \hat{k}\), \(\hat{j}\times\hat{k} = \hat{i}\), and \(\hat{k}\times\hat{i} = \hat{j}\). Reversing any pair changes the sign, for example \(\hat{j}\times\hat{i} = -\hat{k}\).

Practice Problem: A Perpendicular Unit Vector

Find a unit vector perpendicular to both \(\vec{A} = \hat{i} + \hat{j}\) and \(\vec{B} = \hat{j} + \hat{k}\).

Solution

Compute the cross product:

\(\vec{A} \times \vec{B} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = \hat{i}(1 - 0) - \hat{j}(1 - 0) + \hat{k}(1 - 0) = \hat{i} - \hat{j} + \hat{k}\)

The magnitude is \(\sqrt{1^2 + (-1)^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{3}\), so the unit vector is:

\(\hat{n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\hat{i} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\hat{j} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\hat{k}\)

The opposite vector \(-\hat{n}\) is also a valid answer; both directions are perpendicular to the plane containing \(\vec{A}\) and \(\vec{B}\).

1.6.4 Torque: A Cross Product in Action

The torque produced by a force \(\vec{F}\) applied at position \(\vec{r}\) relative to a pivot is:

\(\vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F}\)

Its magnitude is \(\tau = rF\sin\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between the position vector and the force. The direction of the torque is perpendicular to the plane of \(\vec{r}\) and \(\vec{F}\), given by the right-hand rule. A force applied directly toward or away from the pivot produces no torque, because \(\sin\theta = 0\).

Example: Torque About the Origin

A force \(\vec{F} = -3\hat{j} + 4\hat{k}\,\text{N}\) is applied at position \(\vec{r} = 0.5\hat{i} + 0.2\hat{j}\,\text{m}\). Calculate the torque about the origin.

Solution
\(\vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 0.5 & 0.2 & 0 \\ 0 & -3 & 4 \end{vmatrix}\)
\(= \hat{i}\big[(0.2)(4) - (0)(-3)\big] - \hat{j}\big[(0.5)(4) - (0)(0)\big] + \hat{k}\big[(0.5)(-3) - (0.2)(0)\big]\)
\(= 0.8\hat{i} - 2.0\hat{j} - 1.5\hat{k}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}\)

The magnitude is \(\sqrt{0.8^2 + 2.0^2 + 1.5^2} \approx 2.6\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}\). The negative \(z\)-component means the torque tends to rotate the system clockwise when viewed from above.

Key Concept: Dot Versus Cross Product

The dot product answers "how much do these vectors point along each other?" and returns a scalar. The cross product answers "how perpendicular are these vectors, and in what sense?" and returns a vector. Work uses the dot product because only the component of force along the displacement matters; torque uses the cross product because only the perpendicular component of force produces rotation.

1.7 Scalar Triple Product

Three vectors can be combined into a single scalar by taking a dot product with a cross product. The result, \(\vec{A}\cdot(\vec{B}\times\vec{C})\), is called the scalar triple product. Its absolute value is the volume of the parallelepiped whose edges are the three vectors.

Definition: Scalar Triple Product

For vectors \(\vec{A}\), \(\vec{B}\), and \(\vec{C}\), the scalar triple product is

\(\vec{A}\cdot(\vec{B}\times\vec{C})\)

It may be written as \([\vec{A},\vec{B},\vec{C}]\). Its magnitude equals the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the three vectors, and its sign records the orientation (handedness) of the triple.

Because the dot and cross products can be evaluated in either order, the scalar triple product is conveniently written as a determinant:

\(\vec{A}\cdot(\vec{B}\times\vec{C}) = \begin{vmatrix} A_x & A_y & A_z \\ B_x & B_y & B_z \\ C_x & C_y & C_z \end{vmatrix}\)

If the three vectors lie in the same plane (they are coplanar), the parallelepiped is flattened and the volume is zero. Thus a zero scalar triple product is a test for coplanarity.

Figure 1.11: The parallelepiped spanned by \(\vec{A}\), \(\vec{B}\), and \(\vec{C}\). Its volume is the absolute value of the scalar triple product.
Example: Volume of a Parallelepiped

Find the volume of the parallelepiped defined by \(\vec{A} = \hat{i} + 2\hat{j}\), \(\vec{B} = 3\hat{i} - \hat{k}\), and \(\vec{C} = \hat{j} + 2\hat{k}\).

Solution

First compute the cross product:

\(\vec{B} \times \vec{C} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 3 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = \hat{i}(0 + 1) - \hat{j}(6 - 0) + \hat{k}(3 - 0) = \hat{i} - 6\hat{j} + 3\hat{k}\)

Now take the dot product with \(\vec{A}\):

\(\vec{A}\cdot(\vec{B}\times\vec{C}) = (1)(1) + (2)(-6) + (0)(3) = 1 - 12 = -11\)

The negative sign indicates that the triple is left-handed in the order given. The volume is the absolute value:

\(V = |\vec{A}\cdot(\vec{B}\times\vec{C})| = 11\ \text{cubic units}\)
Key Concept: Cyclic Invariance

The scalar triple product is unchanged by a cyclic permutation of the three vectors: \(\vec{A}\cdot(\vec{B}\times\vec{C}) = \vec{B}\cdot(\vec{C}\times\vec{A}) = \vec{C}\cdot(\vec{A}\times\vec{B})\). Swapping any two vectors reverses the sign. This is exactly the behaviour of the determinant when two rows are exchanged.

1.8 Vector Identities and Useful Relations

Several identities recur often enough to be worth memorizing. They follow from the definitions of the dot and cross products and can save a great deal of algebra.

1.8.1 Products of Three Vectors

The vector triple product expands as:

\(\vec{A} \times (\vec{B} \times \vec{C}) = \vec{B}(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{C}) - \vec{C}(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B})\)

The mnemonic is "BAC minus CAB": the middle vector multiplies the dot product of the other two, minus the third vector times the dot product of the first two. Note that the parentheses matter: \(\vec{A}\times(\vec{B}\times\vec{C})\) generally points in the plane of \(\vec{B}\) and \(\vec{C}\), while \((\vec{A}\times\vec{B})\times\vec{C}\) generally points in a different direction.

The Lagrange identity relates the cross product to dot products:

\(|\vec{A}\times\vec{B}|^2 = |\vec{A}|^2|\vec{B}|^2 - (\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B})^2\)

It is the vector form of the trigonometric identity \(\sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos^2\theta\), obtained by dividing by \(A^2B^2\).

Two cross products can be combined using:

\((\vec{A}\times\vec{B})\cdot(\vec{C}\times\vec{D}) = (\vec{A}\cdot\vec{C})(\vec{B}\cdot\vec{D}) - (\vec{A}\cdot\vec{D})(\vec{B}\cdot\vec{C})\)
Example: Checking the Lagrange Identity

Verify the Lagrange identity for \(\vec{A} = 3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j}\) and \(\vec{B} = 4\hat{i} - 3\hat{j}\).

Solution

The two vectors are perpendicular, since \(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B} = 12 - 12 = 0\). Their cross product is:

\(\vec{A}\times\vec{B} = (3)(-3) - (4)(4) = -25\hat{k}\)

so \(|\vec{A}\times\vec{B}|^2 = 625\). On the other side:

\(|\vec{A}|^2|\vec{B}|^2 - (\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B})^2 = (25)(25) - 0 = 625\)

Both sides agree. For perpendicular vectors the identity reduces to \(|\vec{A}\times\vec{B}| = AB\), which is why the magnitude of the cross product equals the product of the magnitudes in this case.

1.8.2 Derivatives of Vector Functions

In later chapters, vectors change with time. The derivative of a vector is taken component by component. The usual product rules carry over, but the order of factors must be preserved for the cross product:

\(\frac{d}{dt}(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}) = \frac{d\vec{A}}{dt}\cdot\vec{B} + \vec{A}\cdot\frac{d\vec{B}}{dt}\)
\(\frac{d}{dt}(\vec{A}\times\vec{B}) = \frac{d\vec{A}}{dt}\times\vec{B} + \vec{A}\times\frac{d\vec{B}}{dt}\)

A particularly useful result follows from \(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{A} = A^2\): differentiating both sides gives \(2\vec{A}\cdot d\vec{A}/dt = 2A\,dA/dt\), or:

\(\vec{A}\cdot\frac{d\vec{A}}{dt} = A\frac{dA}{dt}\)

If the magnitude of a vector is constant, then \(\vec{A}\cdot d\vec{A}/dt = 0\), so the derivative is perpendicular to the vector. This is why the acceleration of an object in uniform circular motion points toward the centre rather than along the velocity: the speed is constant, so the acceleration has no component along the velocity.

Key Concept: Identities Are Shortcuts, Not Magic

Every vector identity can be proved by writing both sides in components. When in doubt, expand in \(\hat{i}\), \(\hat{j}\), \(\hat{k}\) and compare coefficients. The identities are worth learning because they turn long component calculations into a few lines, but they are not additional assumptions about nature.

1.9 Applications of Vectors in Physics

Vector operations are not an abstract preliminary; they are the working tools of physics. The table below summarizes where each operation appears.

Real-World Application

Work and energy. The work done by a force is calculated using the dot product: \(W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d}\). Only the component of force along the displacement transfers energy.

Torque and rotation. The torque produced by a force is calculated using the cross product: \(\vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F}\). The direction of the torque gives the axis of rotation through the right-hand rule.

Electromagnetism. The Lorentz force on a charged particle is given by \(\vec{F} = q(\vec{E} + \vec{v} \times \vec{B})\), which involves both dot and cross products. The magnetic part is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it changes the direction of motion without changing the speed.

Fluid dynamics. The vorticity of a fluid is defined as the curl of the velocity field: \(\vec{\omega} = \nabla \times \vec{v}\). It measures the local rotation of the fluid and is used to model turbulence and weather.

Computer graphics and robotics. Surface normals are found with cross products, and orientations are described with rotation matrices and quaternions. The same vector algebra that describes a force also describes the lighting of a rendered surface.

Example: Magnetic Force Does No Work

A particle with charge \(q\) moves with velocity \(\vec{v}\) in a uniform magnetic field \(\vec{B}\). Show that the magnetic force \(\vec{F} = q\vec{v}\times\vec{B}\) does no work on the particle.

Solution

The power delivered by a force is \(P = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{v}\). Substituting the magnetic force:

\(P = (q\vec{v}\times\vec{B})\cdot\vec{v}\)

The vector \(\vec{v}\times\vec{B}\) is perpendicular to \(\vec{v}\), so its dot product with \(\vec{v}\) is zero:

\((\vec{v}\times\vec{B})\cdot\vec{v} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P = 0\)

The magnetic force can change the direction of the velocity but not its magnitude. The kinetic energy of the particle remains constant.

Vector Application in Static Equilibrium

Equilibrium Analysis Example

A common application of vectors in physics is analyzing forces in static equilibrium. An object is in static equilibrium when it is at rest and the net force acting on it is zero. This means the vector sum of all forces is the zero vector.

For a particle, equilibrium requires \(\sum\vec{F} = \vec{0}\), which is equivalent to three scalar equations, one for each component. For an extended body, the net torque must also vanish: \(\sum\vec{\tau} = \vec{0}\).

Figure 1.12: A traffic light suspended by two cables at \(45^\circ\) and \(30^\circ\) above the horizontal. The three forces on the knot must sum to zero.

Problem: A traffic light with a mass of \(15\,\text{kg}\) is suspended by two cables as shown in the diagram. Find the tension in each cable.

Solution

1. Identify the forces and the condition for equilibrium.

There are three forces acting on the point where the cables meet: the tension from the left cable (\(\vec{T}_1\)), the tension from the right cable (\(\vec{T}_2\)), and the downward force of gravity on the traffic light (\(\vec{W}\)).

The weight of the traffic light is \(W = mg = (15\,\text{kg})(9.8\,\text{m/s}^2) = 147\,\text{N}\). So the weight vector is \(\vec{W} = -147\hat{j}\,\text{N}\).

For the system to be in equilibrium, the vector sum of the forces must be zero:

\(\sum \vec{F} = \vec{T}_1 + \vec{T}_2 + \vec{W} = \vec{0}\)

This vector equation can be broken down into two scalar equations for the \(x\) and \(y\) components:

\(\sum F_x = T_{1x} + T_{2x} + W_x = 0\)
\(\sum F_y = T_{1y} + T_{2y} + W_y = 0\)

2. Resolve each force vector into its components.

Let \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) be the magnitudes of the tension vectors. The cables pull away from the knot, so \(\vec{T}_1\) points up and to the left while \(\vec{T}_2\) points up and to the right.

For \(\vec{T}_1\):

\(T_{1x} = -T_1 \cos 45^\circ, \qquad T_{1y} = T_1 \sin 45^\circ\)

For \(\vec{T}_2\):

\(T_{2x} = T_2 \cos 30^\circ, \qquad T_{2y} = T_2 \sin 30^\circ\)

For \(\vec{W}\):

\(W_x = 0, \qquad W_y = -147\,\text{N}\)

3. Set up and solve the system of equations.

Substitute the components into the equilibrium equations:

\(-T_1 \cos 45^\circ + T_2 \cos 30^\circ = 0\)
\(T_1 \sin 45^\circ + T_2 \sin 30^\circ - 147 = 0\)

From the first equation, express \(T_2\) in terms of \(T_1\):

\(T_2 = T_1 \frac{\cos 45^\circ}{\cos 30^\circ} = T_1 \frac{\sqrt{2}/2}{\sqrt{3}/2} = T_1\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{3}}\)

Substituting into the second equation:

\(T_1 \left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) + T_1 \left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = 147\)
\(T_1 \left(\frac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}}{2\sqrt{3}}\right) = 147 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_1 \approx 131.8\,\text{N}\)

Now find \(T_2\):

\(T_2 = T_1 \frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 107.6\,\text{N}\)

Check. The horizontal components are \(131.8\cos 45^\circ \approx 93.2\,\text{N}\) to the left and \(107.6\cos 30^\circ \approx 93.2\,\text{N}\) to the right, so they cancel. The vertical components are \(131.8\sin 45^\circ \approx 93.2\,\text{N}\) and \(107.6\sin 30^\circ \approx 53.8\,\text{N}\), which add to \(147\,\text{N}\) and balance the weight.

Conclusion: The tension in the left cable is approximately \(131.8\,\text{N}\), and the tension in the right cable is approximately \(107.6\,\text{N}\). The left cable carries the larger tension because it is steeper: it supports a larger share of the vertical load for the same horizontal pull.

Key Concept: Three Forces in Equilibrium

When exactly three non-parallel forces act on an object in equilibrium, their lines of action must meet at a common point, and the vectors must form a closed triangle when placed head to tail. This geometric condition is often faster than solving components, and it provides an immediate check on the signs of the answers.

Problem-Solving Workshop

Physics problems rarely yield to formula hunting. A reliable approach is to translate the words into quantities with units, draw a diagram, choose coordinate axes, and only then select the relevant mathematical tool. For vector problems, this usually means resolving each vector into components before adding, subtracting, or multiplying.

Two checks catch many errors: dimensional consistency and limiting cases. If an equation gives metres per second squared for a speed, or predicts an unreasonable result when a mass becomes very small, stop and re-examine the setup. A third check is to estimate the answer before calculating it; an order-of-magnitude estimate tells you whether a factor-of-ten slip has occurred.

Problem-Solving Checklist
  1. List the known and unknown quantities, including their units.
  2. Sketch the situation and choose a convenient coordinate system.
  3. Convert all data to SI units and carry units through the calculation.
  4. Resolve vectors into components before combining them.
  5. Use the dot product for projections and work; use the cross product for torque, areas, and perpendicular directions.
  6. Check dimensions, signs, significant figures, and limiting cases.
  7. Ask whether the answer is physically reasonable and state any assumptions.
Worked Example: A Complete Vector Analysis

Two vectors are given by \(\vec{A} = 3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j}\) and \(\vec{B} = 4\hat{i} - 3\hat{j}\). Find the dot product, the angle between the vectors, the cross product, the area of the parallelogram they span, and the unit vector in the direction of \(\vec{A}\).

Solution

Dot product.

\(\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = (3)(4) + (4)(-3) = 12 - 12 = 0\)

Because the dot product is zero, the vectors are perpendicular and the angle between them is \(90^\circ\).

Cross product. Both vectors lie in the \(xy\)-plane, so the result has only a \(z\)-component:

\(\vec{A} \times \vec{B} = \big[(3)(-3) - (4)(4)\big]\hat{k} = (-9 - 16)\hat{k} = -25\hat{k}\)

The magnitude is \(25\), which equals the area of the parallelogram formed by the vectors. The negative sign means the rotation from \(\vec{A}\) to \(\vec{B}\) is clockwise when viewed from above.

Unit vector.

\(\hat{A} = \frac{3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j}}{\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2}} = \frac{3}{5}\hat{i} + \frac{4}{5}\hat{j} = 0.6\hat{i} + 0.8\hat{j}\)

Interpretation. If \(\vec{A}\) were a force in newtons and \(\vec{B}\) a displacement in metres, the work done would be zero because the force is perpendicular to the displacement. The cross product would give a torque or an area vector of magnitude \(25\) in the appropriate units. The same pair of vectors therefore illustrates all three products: dot, cross, and unit vector.

Why This Matters

Navigation systems combine displacement vectors from GPS satellites; robotics uses rotation matrices and cross products to describe orientation; and structural engineers resolve forces into components to check whether a bridge or building will remain in equilibrium. The same vector tools recur throughout the course, from projectile motion to electric and magnetic fields.

Key Takeaways

  • A physical quantity is meaningful only when its numerical value and unit are stated together.
  • SI units provide a common language for measurement, while derived units are combinations of base units.
  • Uncertainty and significant figures communicate how precisely a quantity is known; accuracy and precision are different properties.
  • Dimensional analysis and order-of-magnitude estimates are fast ways to catch algebra and unit errors.
  • Scalars have magnitude only; vectors have magnitude and direction, and they are unchanged by translation.
  • Components turn vector addition and multiplication into manageable scalar equations.
  • A unit vector carries direction only; any vector can be written as its magnitude times a unit vector.
  • The dot product is a scalar and vanishes for perpendicular vectors; the cross product is a vector and vanishes for parallel vectors.
  • The magnitude of the cross product is the area of the parallelogram spanned by the vectors.
  • The scalar triple product gives the volume of a parallelepiped and tests whether three vectors are coplanar.
  • Vector identities such as the vector triple product and the Lagrange identity shorten calculations and reveal geometric structure.
  • Equilibrium requires both zero net force and zero net torque; vector components make the conditions solvable.

Chapter Summary

This chapter covered the fundamental concepts required for quantitative physical science: the nature of measurement, units, uncertainty, and the mathematics of vectors. A measurement pairs a number with a unit, and its precision is limited by the instrument and the procedure. Significant figures and uncertainty statements communicate that precision honestly, while dimensional analysis and estimation provide quick checks on any result.

Vectors extend ordinary algebra to quantities with direction. Addition and subtraction are performed geometrically head to tail or algebraically component by component. The dot product produces a scalar that measures projection and underlies work and power; the cross product produces a vector perpendicular to both inputs and underlies torque, angular momentum, and magnetic force. The scalar triple product adds volume and coplanarity, and the standard vector identities organize these operations into a compact toolkit.

Mastery of dimensional analysis, significant figures, and vector operations is essential for success in subsequent topics. In the next chapter, these tools are applied immediately to describe motion along a straight line, where position, velocity, and acceleration are the central quantities.
