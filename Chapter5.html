Chapter 5: Gravitation and Kepler's Laws of Motion

Welcome to Gravitation and Kepler's Laws

In 1687, Isaac Newton published his work on the law of universal gravitation, which revolutionized our understanding of the motion of celestial bodies and completed the framework of classical mechanics. The same force that pulls an apple toward the ground keeps the Moon in orbit and holds the planets in their paths around the Sun.

Gravitation is the weakest of the fundamental forces, yet it is the dominant force on cosmic scales. Newton's law gives the force directly from masses and distance: \(F_g = G\frac{m_1m_2}{r^2}\). Setting that force equal to the centripetal force needed for circular motion turns an orbit into a solvable equation, and Kepler's empirical laws turn observation into a beautiful quantitative pattern.

This chapter combines the dynamics of Chapter 4 with the kinematics of Chapter 3, then adds energy and orbital reasoning that extend circular motion to the solar system.

5.1 Newton's Law of Gravitation

In 1687, Isaac Newton published his work on the law of universal gravitation, which revolutionized our understanding of the motion of celestial bodies and completed the framework of classical mechanics.

Definition

Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation states that every particle in the universe attracts every other particle with a force that is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centers:

\(F_g = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}\)

where:

  • \(F_g\) = gravitational force between the two objects (N)
  • \(G\) = gravitational constant = \(6.674 \times 10^{-11}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2\)
  • \(m_1\), \(m_2\) = masses of the two objects (kg)
  • \(r\) = distance between the centers of the two objects (m)
Key Concept

Key properties of gravitational force:

  • It is always attractive (never repulsive)
  • It acts along the line joining the centers of the two masses
  • It obeys Newton's third law (\(F_{12} = -F_{21}\))
  • It is a conservative force (work done is path-independent)
  • It has infinite range (though it decreases with \(1/r^2\))
Figure 5.1: Gravitational force between two masses
Example

Calculate the gravitational force between Earth and Moon.

\(\text{Mass of Earth } m_E = 5.97 \times 10^{24}\,\text{kg}\)
\(\text{Mass of Moon } m_M = 7.35 \times 10^{22}\,\text{kg}\)
\(\text{Average distance } r = 3.84 \times 10^8\,\text{m}\)
\(F_g = G \frac{m_E m_M}{r^2} = (6.674 \times 10^{-11}) \frac{(5.97 \times 10^{24})(7.35 \times 10^{22})}{(3.84 \times 10^8)^2} \approx 1.98 \times 10^{20}\,\text{N}\)
Practice Problem

Calculate the gravitational force between two 100 kg spheres whose centers are 0.5 m apart.

Solution
\(F_g = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} = (6.674 \times 10^{-11}) \frac{(100)(100)}{(0.5)^2} = (6.674 \times 10^{-11}) \frac{10000}{0.25} = 2.67 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{N}\)

5.1.1 Free-Fall Acceleration and the Gravitational Force

The acceleration due to gravity that we experience on Earth is a result of the gravitational force exerted by Earth on objects near its surface.

Definition

The free-fall acceleration \(g\) at a distance \(r\) from the center of a planet of mass \(M\) is given by:

\(g = \frac{F_g}{m} = \frac{G M}{r^2}\)

where \(m\) is the mass of the falling object.

Key Concept

Important points about free-fall acceleration:

  • It is independent of the mass of the falling object (all objects fall with the same acceleration)
  • It decreases with altitude (\(g \propto 1/r^2\))
  • On Earth's surface (\(r = R_E = 6.37 \times 10^6\) m):
    \(g = \frac{G M_E}{R_E^2} = \frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24})}{(6.37 \times 10^6)^2} \approx 9.80\,\text{m/s}^2\)
  • The value of \(g\) varies slightly with latitude due to Earth's rotation and shape
Figure 5.2: Free-fall acceleration at height h above Earth's surface
Example

Calculate the acceleration due to gravity at an altitude of 400 km above Earth's surface.

Solution
\(R_E = 6.37 \times 10^6\,\text{m}\)
\(h = 400\,\text{km} = 4 \times 10^5\,\text{m}\)
\(r = R_E + h = 6.77 \times 10^6\,\text{m}\)
\(g = \frac{G M_E}{r^2} = \frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24})}{(6.77 \times 10^6)^2} \approx 8.70\,\text{m/s}^2\)
Practice Problem

At what altitude above Earth's surface would the acceleration due to gravity be half its surface value?

Solution
\(g_{\text{surface}} = \frac{G M_E}{R_E^2}\)
\(g_{\text{altitude}} = \frac{G M_E}{r^2} = \frac{1}{2} \frac{G M_E}{R_E^2}\)
\(\frac{1}{r^2} = \frac{1}{2 R_E^2} \;\Rightarrow\; r^2 = 2 R_E^2 \;\Rightarrow\; r = R_E \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414 R_E\)
\(h = r - R_E = R_E(\sqrt{2} - 1) \approx 0.414 R_E \approx 2637\,\text{km}\)

5.1.2 Gravitational Field

Instead of saying that one mass pulls another directly, it is often useful to say that a mass creates a gravitational field in the space around it. The field strength \(\vec{g}\) at a point is the gravitational force per unit mass that a small test mass would experience there:

\(\vec{g} = \frac{\vec{F}_g}{m} = -G\frac{M}{r^2}\hat{r}\)

where \(\hat{r}\) points away from the source mass. The negative sign shows that the field points toward the source. Near Earth's surface, \(|\vec{g}| \approx 9.8\,\text{N/kg} = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2\), which is why the gravitational field strength and the free-fall acceleration have the same value.

Fields from several masses add as vectors. This superposition principle lets us find the net field of a planet and its moon, or of a galaxy, by adding the individual contributions.

Example

At what point on the line between Earth and the Moon is the net gravitational field zero? Use \(M_E = 5.97 \times 10^{24}\,\text{kg}\), \(M_M = 7.35 \times 10^{22}\,\text{kg}\), and \(r = 3.84 \times 10^8\,\text{m}\).

Solution

Let \(x\) be the distance from Earth's centre. At the balance point the field strengths are equal:

\(G\frac{M_E}{x^2} = G\frac{M_M}{(r-x)^2}\)

Taking the square root of both sides gives

\(\frac{\sqrt{M_E}}{x} = \frac{\sqrt{M_M}}{r-x} \quad \Rightarrow \quad x = \frac{\sqrt{M_E}}{\sqrt{M_E}+\sqrt{M_M}}\,r\)

With \(\sqrt{M_E/M_M} \approx \sqrt{81.2} \approx 9.01\),

\(x = \frac{9.01}{9.01+1}(3.84 \times 10^8) \approx 3.46 \times 10^8\,\text{m}\)

The balance point lies about \(3.46 \times 10^8\,\text{m}\) from Earth, roughly \(90\%\) of the way to the Moon.

5.1.3 Gravitational Potential Energy

Gravitational potential energy represents the energy stored in a system of masses due to their gravitational interaction.

Definition

The gravitational potential energy \(U\) between two point masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) separated by distance \(r\) is:

\(U = -G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r}\)

The negative sign indicates that the force is attractive, and the potential energy increases (becomes less negative) as the separation increases.

Key Concept

Key properties of gravitational potential energy:

  • It is always negative (approaching zero as \(r \to \infty\))
  • The zero point is chosen at infinite separation (\(U = 0\) when \(r = \infty\))
  • It represents the work that would be done to separate the masses to infinity
  • For objects near Earth's surface, we can use the approximation \(U = mgh\)
Figure 5.3: Gravitational potential energy as a function of distance
Example

Calculate the gravitational potential energy of a 1000 kg satellite in orbit 400 km above Earth's surface.

Solution
\(M_E = 5.97 \times 10^{24}\,\text{kg}, \quad m = 1000\,\text{kg}\)
\(R_E = 6.37 \times 10^6\,\text{m}, \quad h = 4 \times 10^5\,\text{m}, \quad r = R_E + h = 6.77 \times 10^6\,\text{m}\)
\(U = -G \frac{M_E m}{r} = -(6.674 \times 10^{-11}) \frac{(5.97 \times 10^{24})(1000)}{6.77 \times 10^6} \approx -5.89 \times 10^{10}\,\text{J}\)

5.1.4 Escape Speed

Definition

The escape speed is the minimum speed needed for an object to escape from the gravitational influence of a celestial body without further propulsion. It is given by:

\(v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M}{R}}\)

where:

  • \(M\) = mass of the celestial body
  • \(R\) = radius of the celestial body
Key Concept

Key points about escape speed:

  • It is independent of the mass of the escaping object
  • It depends only on the mass and radius of the celestial body
  • It represents the speed where total energy \(E = K + U = 0\)
  • For Earth: \(v_{\text{esc}} \approx 11.2\) km/s
Escape Speed Derivation

Escape speed derivation:

\(\begin{aligned} E &= K + U = 0 \quad \text{(minimum energy to escape)} \\ \frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{esc}}^2 - G \frac{M m}{R} &= 0 \\ \frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{esc}}^2 &= G \frac{M m}{R} \\ v_{\text{esc}}^2 &= \frac{2 G M}{R} \\ v_{\text{esc}} &= \sqrt{\frac{2 G M}{R}} \end{aligned}\)
Example

Calculate the escape speed from Earth.

Solution
\(M_E = 5.97 \times 10^{24}\,\text{kg}, \quad R_E = 6.37 \times 10^6\,\text{m}\)
\(v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M_E}{R_E}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 (6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24})}{6.37 \times 10^6}} \approx 11{,}200\,\text{m/s} = 11.2\,\text{km/s}\)
Practice Problem

Calculate the escape speed from the Moon (\(M_M = 7.35 \times 10^{22}\) kg, \(R_M = 1.74 \times 10^6\) m).

Solution
\(v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M_M}{R_M}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 (6.674 \times 10^{-11})(7.35 \times 10^{22})}{1.74 \times 10^6}} \approx 2{,}370\,\text{m/s} = 2.37\,\text{km/s}\)

5.1.5 Orbital Energy

A satellite in a circular orbit has both kinetic and potential energy. Combining \(v = \sqrt{GM/r}\) with \(K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2\) gives

\(K = \frac{GMm}{2r}\)

while the potential energy is

\(U = -\frac{GMm}{r}\)

The total mechanical energy is therefore

\(E = K + U = -\frac{GMm}{2r}\)

The negative sign means the satellite is bound to the planet. Adding energy raises the orbit; the satellite must be given energy to move to a larger radius. Escape corresponds to raising the total energy to zero, which reproduces the escape-speed formula derived earlier.

Example

A \(1000\,\text{kg}\) satellite orbits Earth at an altitude of \(400\,\text{km}\). Find its kinetic energy, potential energy, and total mechanical energy.

Solution

The orbital radius is \(r = R_E + h = 6.37 \times 10^6 + 4.00 \times 10^5 = 6.77 \times 10^6\,\text{m}\). Using \(GM_E = 3.985 \times 10^{14}\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2\):

\(U = -\frac{GM_E m}{r} = -\frac{(3.985 \times 10^{14})(1000)}{6.77 \times 10^6} \approx -5.89 \times 10^{10}\,\text{J}\)

For a circular orbit, \(K = -U/2\), so

\(K \approx 2.94 \times 10^{10}\,\text{J}, \qquad E = K + U \approx -2.94 \times 10^{10}\,\text{J}\)

The total energy is negative by exactly the amount of kinetic energy, a general result for circular orbits.

Example

Estimate the escape speed of a spacecraft from Earth's surface, where the radius is \(R_E = 6.37 \times 10^6\,\text{m}\) and the Earth's mass is \(5.97 \times 10^{24}\,\text{kg}\). Use \(G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2\).

Solution

Setting the escape kinetic energy equal to the gravitational potential energy at the surface gives

\(\frac{1}{2}mv_{\text{esc}}^2 = \frac{GM_E m}{R_E}\)

Cancelling the mass \(m\) and rearranging:

\(v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM_E}{R_E}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24})}{6.37 \times 10^6}} = \sqrt{1.25 \times 10^{11}} \approx 1.12 \times 10^5\,\text{m/s} = 11.2\,\text{km/s}\)

The escape speed from Earth is about \(11.2\,\text{km/s}\) (about \(25,000\,\text{mph}\)). A rocket that exceeds this speed does not need to climb out of the gravity well; it has just enough energy to reach infinity with zero speed.

5.2 Kepler's Laws and the Motion of Planets

Johannes Kepler (1571–1630) formulated three empirical laws that describe the motion of planets around the Sun. These laws were later explained by Newton's law of universal gravitation.

5.2.1 Kepler's First Law

Definition

Kepler's First Law (Law of Orbits):

All planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus.

Key Concept

Key points about elliptical orbits:

  • An ellipse has two foci; the Sun is at one focus
  • The semi-major axis \(a\) is half the longest diameter
  • The semi-minor axis \(b\) is half the shortest diameter
  • The eccentricity \(e\) measures how elongated the ellipse is (\(0 \leq e < 1\))
  • For planets, most orbits are nearly circular (\(e \approx 0\))
Figure 5.4: Elliptical orbit described by Kepler's First Law

5.2.2 Kepler's Second Law

Definition

Kepler's Second Law (Law of Areas):

A line joining a planet and the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals of time.

Key Concept

Key points about the Law of Areas:

  • Planets move faster when closer to the Sun (perihelion)
  • Planets move slower when farther from the Sun (aphelion)
  • This is a consequence of conservation of angular momentum
  • The area swept out per unit time is constant

The second law follows from conservation of angular momentum. For a planet of mass \(m\) at position \(\vec{r}\) from the Sun, the angular momentum is \(\vec{L} = \vec{r}\times m\vec{v}\). Because the gravitational force points along \(\vec{r}\), it exerts no torque about the Sun, so \(\vec{L}\) is constant. In a short time \(dt\), the line from the Sun sweeps out a small triangle of area

\(dA = \tfrac{1}{2}|\vec{r}\times\vec{v}|\,dt = \frac{L}{2m}dt\)

Since \(L\) and \(m\) are constant, \(dA/dt = L/(2m)\) is constant. A planet therefore moves faster near perihelion, where \(r\) is small, and slower near aphelion, where \(r\) is large.

Figure 5.5: Kepler's Second Law — equal areas in equal times

5.2.3 Kepler's Third Law

Definition

Kepler's Third Law (Law of Periods):

The square of the orbital period of a planet is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit.

Mathematically:

\(T^2 \propto a^3 \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{T^2}{a^3} = \text{constant}\)

For planets orbiting the Sun, the constant is the same for all planets.

Key Concept

Key points about the Law of Periods:

  • It relates orbital period to orbital size
  • Planets farther from the Sun have longer orbital periods
  • Newton showed that the constant is \(\frac{4\pi^2}{G M_S}\), where \(M_S\) is the Sun's mass
  • The exact form is: \(T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G M_S} a^3\)
Example

Calculate the orbital period of Earth around the Sun.

Solution
\(M_S = 1.99 \times 10^{30}\,\text{kg}\)
\(a = 1.496 \times 10^{11}\,\text{m} \quad \text{(1 astronomical unit)}\)
\(T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G M_S} a^3\)
\(T = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2 (1.496 \times 10^{11})^3}{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(1.99 \times 10^{30})}} \approx 3.156 \times 10^7\,\text{s} \approx 365.25\,\text{days}\)
Practice Problem

Mars has an orbital semi-major axis of 1.524 AU. Calculate its orbital period in Earth years.

Solution

Using Kepler's Third Law:

\(\frac{T_M^2}{a_M^3} = \frac{T_E^2}{a_E^3} \;\Rightarrow\; T_M = T_E \left( \frac{a_M}{a_E} \right)^{3/2} = 1 \times (1.524)^{3/2} \approx 1.88\,\text{years}\)
Kepler's Laws Summary
  1. First Law (Orbits): Planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus.
  2. Second Law (Areas): A line joining a planet and the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times.
  3. Third Law (Periods): The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis:
    \(T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G M} a^3\)

Problem-Solving Workshop

Orbital problems are circular-motion problems in disguise. The gravitational force supplies the centripetal force, so the satellite's mass cancels from the speed equation. The first step is always to convert an altitude into an orbital radius by adding Earth's radius; using \(h\) in place of \(r\) is the most common error in this chapter.

Write the force equation \(G M m/r^2 = mv^2/r\), cancel \(m\) and one factor of \(r\), and solve for the speed. The period then follows from the circumference divided by the speed, or from Kepler's third law. Check that the result is reasonable: low-Earth-orbit periods are about 90 to 100 minutes, while geostationary periods are about 24 hours.

Problem-Solving Checklist
  1. Identify the central body and its mass \(M\), and convert altitude to \(r = R + h\).
  2. State the condition for circular orbit: gravitational force equals the required centripetal force.
  3. Write \(G M m/r^2 = mv^2/r\) and cancel the satellite mass \(m\).
  4. Solve for the orbital speed \(v = \sqrt{GM/r}\).
  5. Find the period from \(T = 2\pi r/v\), or use \(T^2 = 4\pi^2 r^3/(GM)\).
  6. Check units and compare the period with known orbits such as the ISS or a geostationary satellite.
Worked Example

A remote-sensing satellite moves in a circular orbit \(800\,\text{km}\) above Earth's surface. Using \(M_E = 5.97 \times 10^{24}\,\text{kg}\), \(R_E = 6.37 \times 10^6\,\text{m}\), and \(G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2\), find its orbital speed and orbital period.

Solution

Orbital radius. The altitude must be added to Earth's radius:

\(r = R_E + h = 6.37 \times 10^6 + 8.00 \times 10^5 = 7.17 \times 10^6\,\text{m}\)

Orbital speed. Gravity provides the centripetal force:

\(G\frac{M_E m}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r} \quad \Rightarrow \quad v = \sqrt{\frac{GM_E}{r}}\)
\(v = \sqrt{\frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24})}{7.17 \times 10^6}} = \sqrt{5.56 \times 10^7} = 7.45 \times 10^3\,\text{m/s}\)

Orbital period. One revolution covers \(2\pi r\):

\(T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi(7.17 \times 10^6)}{7.45 \times 10^3} = 6.04 \times 10^3\,\text{s}\)

Converting to minutes and hours:

\(T \approx 101\,\text{min} \approx 1.68\,\text{h}\)

The satellite travels at about \(7.45\,\text{km/s}\) and completes one orbit in about \(101\) minutes, which is typical of a low-Earth orbit.

Why This Matters

Earth-observation and imaging satellites are often placed in low orbits so that their cameras are close enough to resolve small features. An \(800\,\text{km}\) orbit gives a period of about \(101\) minutes, so the satellite circles Earth roughly fourteen times per day and its ground track shifts westward each pass. Mission planners use exactly this calculation to choose an altitude that balances image resolution against revisit time.

Key Takeaways

  • Newton's law of gravitation is an inverse-square law: \(F_g = Gm_1m_2/r^2\).
  • The gravitational field strength near a body is \(g = GM/r^2\), measured outward from its centre.
  • Gravitational potential energy is negative when the zero is set at infinite separation.
  • Escape speed is \(v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2GM/R}\) and is independent of the escaping object's mass.
  • Kepler's laws describe elliptical orbits, equal areas in equal times, and \(T^2 \propto a^3\).
  • For a circular orbit, \(v = \sqrt{GM/r}\) and \(T = 2\pi r/v\), with \(r = R + h\).
  • The mass of the orbiting satellite cancels out of the orbital speed and period.
