Chapter 2: Kinematics in One Dimension

Welcome to Kinematics

Kinematics is the branch of physics that describes how objects move without asking why they move. In one-dimensional kinematics the object is constrained to a straight line, so a single coordinate \(x\) is enough to specify its position at every instant.

In this chapter you will build a precise language for motion: position, distance, displacement, speed, velocity, and acceleration. You will learn to read motion graphs, apply the kinematic equations for constant acceleration, analyse free fall, and compare measurements made in different frames of reference.

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish between distance and displacement.
  • Differentiate between speed and velocity.
  • Calculate average and instantaneous velocity.
  • Explain acceleration and its relationship to velocity.
  • Solve problems involving motion with constant acceleration.
  • Analyse free-fall motion under gravity.
  • Interpret position-time, velocity-time, and acceleration-time graphs.
  • Apply kinematic equations and relative-velocity reasoning to real-world problems.

2.1 Distance and Displacement

Every measurement of motion begins with a choice of coordinate system. Once an origin and a positive direction are fixed, the position of an object is a single signed number. From position we build two closely related but distinct ideas: distance and displacement.

Position and Reference Frame

Definition: Position and Reference Frame

Position is the location of an object relative to a chosen origin, together with its direction. In one dimension it is written as a signed coordinate \(x\).

A reference frame (or frame of reference) is the coordinate system, including its origin and positive direction, from which positions and velocities are measured. A measurement of position or velocity has meaning only after the reference frame has been stated.

Figure 2.1: A one-dimensional coordinate system. The origin is at \(x = 0\), the positive direction points to the right, and point \(P\) is at \(x = +6\ \text{m}\).

Distance

Definition: Distance

Distance is a scalar quantity equal to the total length of the path travelled by an object, regardless of direction. It is always positive or zero and is measured in units of length, such as metres (\(\text{m}\)).

Example 2.1: Total Distance Walked

A person walks \(5\ \text{m}\) to the east, turns around, and walks \(3\ \text{m}\) to the west. The total distance travelled is the sum of the lengths of both parts of the path:

\(\text{Distance} = 5\ \text{m} + 3\ \text{m} = 8\ \text{m}\)

Distance records how much ground was covered, not where the person ended up.

Displacement

Definition: Displacement

Displacement is a vector quantity equal to the change in position of an object. It is the straight-line distance and direction from the initial position to the final position:

\(\Delta x = x_f - x_i\)

The sign of \(\Delta x\) gives the direction of the displacement along the chosen axis.

Example 2.2: Displacement for the Same Trip

Using the same walk, let the starting point be \(x_i = 0\). After walking \(5\ \text{m}\) east the person is at \(x = +5\ \text{m}\); after walking \(3\ \text{m}\) west the final position is \(x_f = +2\ \text{m}\). Therefore

\(\Delta x = x_f - x_i = (+2\ \text{m}) - (0\ \text{m}) = +2\ \text{m}\)

The positive sign means the net change in position is \(2\ \text{m}\) to the east. The person walked \(8\ \text{m}\) but is only \(2\ \text{m}\) from the starting point.

Practice 2.1

A car travels \(8\ \text{km}\) north, then \(12\ \text{km}\) south, and finally \(5\ \text{km}\) north again.

  1. What is the total distance travelled?
  2. What is the final displacement from the starting point?
Solution
  1. Total distance \(= 8\ \text{km} + 12\ \text{km} + 5\ \text{km} = 25\ \text{km}\).
  2. Take north as positive. Then
    \(\begin{aligned} \Delta x &= (+8\ \text{km}) + (-12\ \text{km}) + (+5\ \text{km})\\ &= +1\ \text{km} \end{aligned}\)
    The displacement is \(1\ \text{km}\) north.
Why This Matters

A car's odometer records distance, while a GPS receiver reports displacement between two points. On a winding mountain road the two numbers can differ greatly, which is why navigation systems distinguish between the distance driven and the straight-line separation of the endpoints.

2.2 Speed and Velocity

Distance and displacement tell us how far something moved. Speed and velocity tell us how quickly that change occurred. As with distance and displacement, one is a scalar and the other is a vector.

Speed and Average Speed

Definition: Speed and Average Speed

Speed is a scalar quantity that describes how fast an object is moving. It is the rate at which distance is covered.

Average speed is the total distance travelled divided by the total time elapsed:

\(\text{Average speed} = \dfrac{\text{Total distance}}{\text{Total time}}\)

The SI unit of speed is metres per second (\(\text{m/s}\)), although \(\text{km/h}\) is common in everyday life.

Example 2.3: Average Speed of a Car

A car travels a total distance of \(300\ \text{km}\) in \(4\ \text{h}\). Its average speed is

\(\text{Average speed} = \dfrac{300\ \text{km}}{4\ \text{h}} = 75\ \text{km/h}\)

This is the rate at which distance is covered; it says nothing about the direction of travel.

Velocity and Average Velocity

Definition: Velocity and Average Velocity

Velocity is a vector quantity that describes the rate of change of displacement. It specifies both how fast an object moves and in which direction.

Average velocity is the total displacement divided by the time interval over which that displacement occurred:

\(\bar{v} = \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{x_f - x_i}{t_f - t_i}\)
Example 2.4: Average Velocity

A car moves from \(x_i = 10\ \text{m}\) to \(x_f = 50\ \text{m}\) in \(5\ \text{s}\). Its average velocity is

\(\bar{v} = \dfrac{50\ \text{m} - 10\ \text{m}}{5\ \text{s}} = \dfrac{40\ \text{m}}{5\ \text{s}} = +8\ \text{m/s}\)

The positive sign shows that the car moves in the \(+x\) direction.

Key Concept: Speed versus Velocity

Speed and velocity have the same magnitude only when the motion is in one direction along a straight line. If an object reverses direction, its average speed and the magnitude of its average velocity can be very different.

Instantaneous Velocity

Definition: Instantaneous Velocity

Instantaneous velocity is the velocity of an object at one specific moment. It is the limit of the average velocity as the time interval becomes infinitesimally small:

\(v(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{dx}{dt}\)

Graphically, the instantaneous velocity at a point is the slope of the tangent to the position-time graph at that point.

Figure 2.3: Instantaneous velocity is the slope of the tangent to the position-time curve. As the interval \(\Delta t\) shrinks, the chord approaches the tangent at \(P\).
Practice 2.2: Average and Instantaneous Velocity

The position of a particle is given by \(x(t) = 2t^2 + 3t + 1\), where \(x\) is in metres and \(t\) is in seconds.

  1. Find the average velocity between \(t = 1\ \text{s}\) and \(t = 3\ \text{s}\).
  2. Find the instantaneous velocity at \(t = 2\ \text{s}\).
Solution
  1. At \(t_i = 1\ \text{s}\): \(x(1) = 2(1)^2 + 3(1) + 1 = 6\ \text{m}\).
    At \(t_f = 3\ \text{s}\): \(x(3) = 2(3)^2 + 3(3) + 1 = 28\ \text{m}\).
    \(\bar{v} = \dfrac{28 - 6}{3 - 1} = \dfrac{22\ \text{m}}{2\ \text{s}} = 11\ \text{m/s}\).
  2. Differentiate the position function:
    \(\begin{aligned} v(t) &= \dfrac{dx}{dt} = 4t + 3\\ v(2) &= 4(2) + 3 = 11\ \text{m/s} \end{aligned}\)

The average velocity over the two-second interval happens to equal the instantaneous velocity at its midpoint because the acceleration is constant.

Why This Matters

A speedometer shows instantaneous speed, but it does not show direction. A navigation app that reports "12 m/s north-east" is reporting a velocity. Traffic enforcement, aircraft navigation, and sports analytics all depend on distinguishing the two.

2.3 Acceleration

Velocity can change in magnitude, in direction, or in both. The quantity that measures how quickly velocity changes is acceleration.

Definition: Average and Instantaneous Acceleration

Acceleration is the vector quantity that describes the rate of change of velocity. An object accelerates if its speed changes, its direction changes, or both.

Average acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time interval:

\(\bar{a} = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{v_f - v_i}{t_f - t_i}\)

Instantaneous acceleration is the limit of the average acceleration as the time interval approaches zero:

\(a(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{dv}{dt} = \dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}\)

The SI unit of acceleration is metres per second squared (\(\text{m/s}^2\)).

Key Concept: Reading the Sign of Acceleration
  • Same signs: when velocity and acceleration have the same sign, the object speeds up.
  • Opposite signs: when velocity and acceleration have opposite signs, the object slows down. This is often called deceleration.
  • Zero acceleration: an object moving with constant velocity has zero acceleration.
  • At a turning point: velocity may be zero while acceleration is not. A ball at the top of its flight has \(v = 0\) but \(a = -9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\).
Example 2.5: Calculating Acceleration

A cyclist increases speed from \(4.0\ \text{m/s}\) to \(10.0\ \text{m/s}\) in \(3.0\ \text{s}\) while moving in the \(+x\) direction. The average acceleration is

\(\bar{a} = \dfrac{10.0\ \text{m/s} - 4.0\ \text{m/s}}{3.0\ \text{s}} = +2.0\ \text{m/s}^2\)

The positive sign means the acceleration points in the same direction as the velocity, so the cyclist speeds up.

Figure 2.4: On a velocity-time graph, the slope of the line gives the acceleration. A straight line means constant acceleration.
Practice 2.3: Acceleration of a Car

A car travelling at \(12\ \text{m/s}\) accelerates uniformly to \(30\ \text{m/s}\) in \(6.0\ \text{s}\).

  1. Find its acceleration.
  2. If the acceleration stays constant, what is its velocity after another \(4.0\ \text{s}\)?
Solution
  1. The acceleration is
    \(a = \dfrac{30 - 12}{6.0} = 3.0\ \text{m/s}^2\)
  2. Using \(a = \Delta v/\Delta t\) over the next \(4.0\ \text{s}\):
    \(v = 30 + (3.0)(4.0) = 42\ \text{m/s}\)

2.4 Motion with Constant Acceleration

A very common and important type of one-dimensional motion is motion with constant acceleration. In that case the average acceleration and the instantaneous acceleration are equal, and the motion can be described by a compact set of equations called the kinematic equations.

The Kinematic Equations (for constant \(a\))

For an object that starts at position \(x_0\) with velocity \(v_0\) and moves with constant acceleration \(a\):

EquationRelates
\(v = v_0 + at\)velocity, acceleration, and time
\(x = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2\)position, acceleration, and time
\(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)\)velocity and displacement
\(x = x_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}(v_0 + v)t\)position and average velocity

Each equation omits one variable. Choose the equation that contains the unknown you need and does not contain the quantity you neither know nor want.

Deriving the Kinematic Equations

The equations are not independent facts to memorise; they follow from the definitions of velocity and acceleration when \(a\) is constant. Starting from \(a = dv/dt\) and integrating gives

\(v(t) = v_0 + at\)

Substituting this expression into \(v = dx/dt\) and integrating from \(0\) to \(t\) gives

\(x(t) = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2\)

Eliminating \(t\) between these two equations yields

\(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)\)

Finally, because the velocity changes linearly with time, the average velocity is the arithmetic mean of the initial and final velocities, \(\bar{v} = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_0 + v)\), which gives the fourth form \(x = x_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}(v_0 + v)t\).

Key Concept: When the Equations Apply

The kinematic equations are valid only while the acceleration is constant. If the acceleration changes, split the motion into intervals over which it is approximately constant, or use calculus with the actual \(a(t)\). Never mix quantities from two different stages in a single application of the equations.

Example 2.6: A Train Accelerating Uniformly

A train is moving at \(20\ \text{m/s}\) when it begins to accelerate uniformly at \(1.5\ \text{m/s}^2\).

  1. How long does it take to reach \(35\ \text{m/s}\)?
  2. How far does it travel during this time?

Given: \(v_0 = 20\ \text{m/s}\), \(a = 1.5\ \text{m/s}^2\), \(v = 35\ \text{m/s}\).

  1. Use \(v = v_0 + at\):
    \(35 = 20 + 1.5t \quad\Rightarrow\quad t = \dfrac{15}{1.5} = 10\ \text{s}\)
  2. Use \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x\), which does not require the time:
    \(\begin{aligned} (35)^2 &= (20)^2 + 2(1.5)\Delta x\\ 1225 &= 400 + 3\Delta x \end{aligned}\)
    \(\Delta x = \dfrac{825}{3} = 275\ \text{m}\)
Practice 2.4: Starting from Rest

A car starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at \(2.5\ \text{m/s}^2\) for \(8.0\ \text{s}\). Find its final velocity and the distance it covers.

Solution

With \(v_0 = 0\), \(a = 2.5\ \text{m/s}^2\), and \(t = 8.0\ \text{s}\):

\(v = v_0 + at = 0 + (2.5)(8.0) = 20\ \text{m/s}\)
\(\Delta x = v_0t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 = 0 + \tfrac{1}{2}(2.5)(8.0)^2 = 80\ \text{m}\)

The same displacement follows from \(\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(0 + 20)(8.0) = 80\ \text{m}\).

Why This Matters

Runway lengths, elevator travel times, and the distance needed to merge onto a highway are all calculated with the constant-acceleration equations. Engineers use \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x\) to size braking systems and safety zones because it links speed and distance without requiring a stopwatch.

2.5 Free Fall Motion

Near the surface of the Earth, an object dropped or thrown vertically moves with an almost constant downward acceleration once air resistance is neglected. This special case of constant acceleration is called free fall.

Definition: Free Fall

Free fall is the motion of an object under the influence of gravity alone, with air resistance neglected. Near the Earth's surface every freely falling object has the same constant downward acceleration, called the acceleration due to gravity:

\(g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2 \quad (\text{approximately } 32\ \text{ft/s}^2)\)

When upward is chosen as positive, the acceleration of a freely falling object is \(a = -g = -9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\).

Key Concept: Working with Free Fall
  • Use the standard kinematic equations with \(a = -g\) when upward is positive.
  • An object thrown upward has a positive initial velocity that decreases to zero at its maximum height.
  • The acceleration remains \(-9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\) even at the top of the trajectory, where the velocity is momentarily zero.
  • The motion is symmetric when the object returns to its starting height: the upward and downward travel times are equal, and the return speed equals the launch speed.
  • Mass does not appear in the equations; in the absence of air resistance, all objects fall with the same acceleration.
Figure 2.5: A ball thrown upward at \(20\ \text{m/s}\). The velocity becomes zero at the top, but the acceleration is \(-9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\) throughout the motion.
Example 2.7: A Ball Thrown Upward

A ball is thrown vertically upward with an initial velocity of \(20\ \text{m/s}\).

  1. How high does it rise?
  2. How long does it take to reach its highest point?
  3. What is the total time in the air before it returns to the starting point?

Given: \(v_0 = +20\ \text{m/s}\), \(a = -9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\). At the highest point \(v = 0\).

  1. Use the equation without time:
    \(0 = (20)^2 + 2(-9.8)\Delta y \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta y = \dfrac{400}{19.6} \approx 20.4\ \text{m}\)
  2. Use \(v = v_0 + at\):
    \(0 = 20 - 9.8t \quad\Rightarrow\quad t = \dfrac{20}{9.8} \approx 2.04\ \text{s}\)
  3. By symmetry, the total time is twice the time to the top:
    \(t_{\text{total}} = 2(2.04) \approx 4.08\ \text{s}\)
Example 2.8: A Stone Thrown from a Cliff

A stone is thrown vertically upward with a speed of \(12\ \text{m/s}\) from the edge of a \(30\ \text{m}\) cliff. Neglecting air resistance, find the time until it reaches the ground and its speed just before impact.

Take upward as positive and place the origin at the base of the cliff, so \(y_0 = +30\ \text{m}\), \(v_0 = +12\ \text{m/s}\), and \(a = -9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\). At the ground \(y = 0\):

\(0 = 30 + 12t - 4.9t^2\)

Rearranging gives \(4.9t^2 - 12t - 30 = 0\). The quadratic formula yields

\(\begin{aligned} t &= \dfrac{12 + \sqrt{(-12)^2 - 4(4.9)(-30)}}{2(4.9)}\\ &= \dfrac{12 + \sqrt{732}}{9.8} \approx 3.98\ \text{s} \end{aligned}\)

The impact velocity follows from \(v = v_0 + at\):

\(v = 12 - (9.8)(3.98) \approx -27.0\ \text{m/s}\)

The negative sign means the stone is moving downward at about \(27\ \text{m/s}\). The stone first rises for about \(1.2\ \text{s}\) before reversing direction; the quadratic automatically accounts for that part of the motion.

Practice 2.5: Dropped from Rest

A ball is dropped from rest and falls for \(2.5\ \text{s}\) before hitting the ground. How far does it fall, and what is its speed at impact?

Solution

Take downward as positive, so \(v_0 = 0\) and \(a = +9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\). The fall distance is

\(\Delta y = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(9.8)(2.5)^2 \approx 30.6\ \text{m}\)

The impact speed is

\(v = at = (9.8)(2.5) = 24.5\ \text{m/s}\)

Equivalently, \(v = \sqrt{2(9.8)(30.6)} \approx 24.5\ \text{m/s}\).

Why This Matters

Free-fall equations describe the motion of a dropped phone, a diver, and a rocket coasting after its engines cut off (while gravity is the only significant force). They also explain why reaction-time tests use a falling ruler: the distance the ruler drops is a direct measure of the time delay.

2.6 Graphical Analysis of Motion

Graphs are a powerful tool for visualising and analysing motion. The three main motion graphs are connected: the slope of one graph gives the quantity plotted on the next, and the area under one graph gives a change in the next.

Interpreting Motion Graphs
  • Position vs. time (\(x\)-\(t\)) graph
    • The slope at any point gives the instantaneous velocity.
    • A straight line means constant velocity; a curved line means acceleration.
    • A horizontal line means the object is at rest.
  • Velocity vs. time (\(v\)-\(t\)) graph
    • The slope gives the acceleration.
    • A straight line means constant acceleration.
    • A horizontal line means constant velocity and zero acceleration.
    • The area under the curve between two times gives the displacement \(\Delta x\).
  • Acceleration vs. time (\(a\)-\(t\)) graph
    • The area under the curve between two times gives the change in velocity \(\Delta v\).
Figure 2.6: Relationships between the motion graphs for constant positive acceleration. The \(x\)-\(t\) curve steepens, the \(v\)-\(t\) line has constant slope, and the \(a\)-\(t\) graph is horizontal.

Example 2.9: A Three-Stage Trip

An object's velocity rises uniformly from rest to \(12\ \text{m/s}\) in \(4.0\ \text{s}\), remains constant at \(12\ \text{m/s}\) for \(6.0\ \text{s}\), and then decreases uniformly to rest in \(3.0\ \text{s}\). Find the acceleration in each stage and the total displacement.

The acceleration is the slope of each straight segment:

\(\begin{aligned} a_1 &= \dfrac{12 - 0}{4.0} = 3.0\ \text{m/s}^2\\ a_2 &= 0\\ a_3 &= \dfrac{0 - 12}{3.0} = -4.0\ \text{m/s}^2 \end{aligned}\)

The displacement is the area under the velocity-time graph. The three areas are

\(\begin{aligned} \Delta x_1 &= \tfrac{1}{2}(4.0)(12) = 24\ \text{m}\\ \Delta x_2 &= (6.0)(12) = 72\ \text{m}\\ \Delta x_3 &= \tfrac{1}{2}(3.0)(12) = 18\ \text{m} \end{aligned}\)

Thus \(\Delta x_{\text{total}} = 24 + 72 + 18 = 114\ \text{m}\). The average velocity for the whole trip is \(114/(4.0 + 6.0 + 3.0) \approx 8.8\ \text{m/s}\), which is not the simple average of the three stage velocities because the stages last for different times.

Figure 2.7: The velocity-time graph for Example 2.9. The shaded areas represent the displacements during acceleration, constant velocity, and braking.
Practice 2.6: Area under a Velocity-Time Graph

A car accelerates uniformly from rest to \(15\ \text{m/s}\) in \(5.0\ \text{s}\), travels at \(15\ \text{m/s}\) for \(10.0\ \text{s}\), and then brakes uniformly to rest in \(5.0\ \text{s}\). Find the total displacement of the car.

Solution

Compute the three areas under the \(v\)-\(t\) graph:

\(\begin{aligned} \Delta x_1 &= \tfrac{1}{2}(5.0)(15) = 37.5\ \text{m}\\ \Delta x_2 &= (10.0)(15) = 150\ \text{m}\\ \Delta x_3 &= \tfrac{1}{2}(5.0)(15) = 37.5\ \text{m} \end{aligned}\)
\(\Delta x_{\text{total}} = 37.5 + 150 + 37.5 = 225\ \text{m}\)
Why This Matters

Vehicle data loggers, fitness trackers, and rocket telemetry all record motion as graphs. Reading slope and area lets an engineer estimate acceleration and distance travelled without integrating the raw data by hand, and it makes anomalies such as sudden braking or wheel slip immediately visible.

2.7 Relative Velocity in One Dimension

Velocity is always measured with respect to some frame of reference. A passenger walking down a moving train has one velocity relative to the train and a different velocity relative to the ground. Relative-velocity reasoning lets us convert between such descriptions.

Definition: Relative Velocity

Relative velocity is the velocity of an object as observed from a particular frame of reference. If object A has velocity \(v_A\) and object B has velocity \(v_B\) with respect to the ground, then the velocity of A relative to B is

\(v_{A/B} = v_A - v_B\)

Similarly, the velocity of B relative to A is \(v_{B/A} = v_B - v_A = -v_{A/B}\).

Key Concept: Solving Relative-Velocity Problems
  • Choose a consistent coordinate system and state which direction is positive.
  • Identify all velocities with respect to the same reference frame, usually the ground.
  • Apply \(v_{A/B} = v_A - v_B\) carefully, keeping the order of the subscripts.
  • Pay close attention to signs: velocities in opposite directions have opposite signs.
  • When velocities are given relative to different frames, add or subtract them to bring them into a common frame first.
Figure 2.8: Both cars move east, but Car B gains on Car A at \(20\ \text{km/h}\). In Car A's frame, Car B moves east at \(20\ \text{km/h}\).
Example 2.10: Two Cars on the Same Road

Car A travels east at \(60\ \text{km/h}\) and Car B travels east at \(80\ \text{km/h}\), both relative to the ground. What is the velocity of Car B relative to Car A?

Take east as positive: \(v_{B/\text{ground}} = +80\ \text{km/h}\) and \(v_{A/\text{ground}} = +60\ \text{km/h}\). Then

\(v_{B/A} = v_{B/\text{ground}} - v_{A/\text{ground}} = 80 - 60 = +20\ \text{km/h}\)

Car B moves at \(20\ \text{km/h}\) east relative to Car A.

Practice 2.7: Walking on a Train

A train moves east at \(50\ \text{m/s}\) relative to the ground. A passenger walks toward the front of the train at \(2\ \text{m/s}\) relative to the train. What is the passenger's velocity relative to the ground?

Solution

Let east be positive. Then \(v_{\text{train/ground}} = +50\ \text{m/s}\) and \(v_{\text{passenger/train}} = +2\ \text{m/s}\). The passenger's velocity relative to the ground is the sum:

\(\begin{aligned} v_{\text{passenger/ground}} &= v_{\text{passenger/train}} + v_{\text{train/ground}}\\ &= 2 + 50 = +52\ \text{m/s} \end{aligned}\)

The passenger moves at \(52\ \text{m/s}\) east relative to the ground.

Why This Matters

Relative velocity explains why two trains passing in opposite directions seem to flash by much faster than either one moves relative to the ground. Air-traffic control, ship navigation, and the design of overtaking lanes all rely on the same subtraction of velocities.

Problem-Solving Workshop

Most one-dimensional kinematics problems become manageable once you decide which part of the motion is uniform and which part is accelerated. A driver's reaction interval is constant-velocity motion, while the braking interval is constant-acceleration motion. Treating them as one motion with a single acceleration is a common source of error.

Begin by drawing a motion diagram or a simple sketch and marking the positive direction. Write every known quantity with its sign, because a negative acceleration does not always mean slowing down. Then apply the kinematic equations separately to each stage and combine only the final displacements and times.

Problem-Solving Checklist
  1. Sketch the motion and choose a positive direction; state the sign convention explicitly.
  2. List the initial and final positions, velocities, accelerations, and times for each stage, with signs.
  3. Identify intervals of constant velocity and intervals of constant acceleration.
  4. Use \(x = x_0 + v_0t\) for a reaction or constant-velocity stage.
  5. Apply \(v = v_0 + at\), \(x = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2\), and \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x\) within one stage only.
  6. Check that the signs, units, and total time are physically reasonable.
Example 2.11: Reaction Time and Braking

A car travels at \(25\ \text{m/s}\) on a straight road. The driver sees an obstacle and takes \(0.70\ \text{s}\) to react before braking. The brakes then produce a constant acceleration of magnitude \(6.0\ \text{m/s}^2\) opposite to the motion. Find the total stopping distance and the total time from seeing the obstacle to stopping.

Take the direction of motion as positive, so \(v_0 = +25\ \text{m/s}\) and the braking acceleration is \(a = -6.0\ \text{m/s}^2\).

Stage 1: reaction. The car moves at constant velocity for \(t_1 = 0.70\ \text{s}\):

\(\Delta x_1 = v_0t_1 = (25)(0.70) = 17.5\ \text{m}\)

Stage 2: braking. The final velocity is \(v = 0\). The braking time follows from \(v = v_0 + at\):

\(t_2 = \dfrac{v - v_0}{a} = \dfrac{0 - 25}{-6.0} = 4.17\ \text{s}\)

The braking distance follows from \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x_2\):

\(\Delta x_2 = \dfrac{0 - (25)^2}{2(-6.0)} = \dfrac{625}{12.0} = 52.1\ \text{m}\)

The total stopping distance and time are

\(\begin{aligned} \Delta x_{\text{total}} &= 17.5 + 52.1 = 69.6\ \text{m}\\ t_{\text{total}} &= 0.70 + 4.17 = 4.87\ \text{s} \end{aligned}\)

The car stops after about \(70\ \text{m}\) and \(4.9\ \text{s}\). It would not stop within \(60\ \text{m}\), so a driver must anticipate obstacles well beyond the braking distance alone.

Why This Matters

Road-safety design uses exactly this two-stage calculation. A following distance that ignores reaction time is too short, because at \(25\ \text{m/s}\) the car covers \(17.5\ \text{m}\) before the brakes even engage. The same reasoning sets stopping sight distances on highways and explains why tired or distracted drivers need much larger gaps.

Key Takeaways

  • Kinematic equations apply only while the acceleration is constant; treat reaction and braking as separate stages.
  • The sign of velocity and the sign of acceleration must be chosen from the same coordinate direction.
  • During a reaction time the vehicle travels at constant velocity, so \(d_{\text{reaction}} = vt\).
  • For constant acceleration, \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x\) is often the fastest route when time is unknown.
  • Displacement is a vector; distance is the total path length and is always positive.
  • On a velocity-time graph, the slope gives acceleration and the area gives displacement.
  • Free fall is constant acceleration with \(a = -g\) when upward is positive.
  • Relative velocities are found by subtracting the velocity of the observer from the velocity of the object.

Chapter Summary

This chapter introduced the fundamental concepts of kinematics: position, displacement, velocity, and acceleration. We distinguished between scalar quantities (distance and speed) and vector quantities (displacement, velocity, and acceleration). A key focus was the set of kinematic equations that govern motion under constant acceleration, a condition that applies to many real-world situations, including free fall under gravity. We also explored relative velocity in one dimension and the powerful technique of graphical analysis for understanding the relationships between position, velocity, and acceleration over time.
