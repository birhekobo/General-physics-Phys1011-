Chapter 3: Kinematics in Two Dimensions

Welcome to Two-Dimensional Kinematics

In one dimension, a position is a single coordinate and a motion is a straight line. In two dimensions, position, velocity, and acceleration become vectors that live in a plane. That is why this chapter is where vectors stop being a preliminary and become the main character of the mathematics.

A position vector \(\vec{r}\) describes a point through its components along two perpendicular axes: \(\vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j}\). Velocity is the rate of change of position, \(\vec{v} = \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} = v_x\hat{i} + v_y\hat{j}\), and acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, \(\vec{a} = \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = a_x\hat{i} + a_y\hat{j}\).

The key idea is that the horizontal and vertical components are independent one-dimensional problems glued together by time. Once each component is handled, the full vector can be reconstructed with components and magnitudes.

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Extend kinematic concepts from one dimension to two dimensions

  • Analyze projectile motion and solve related problems

3.1 Displacement, Velocity and Acceleration in Two Dimensions

In two-dimensional kinematics, we extend the concepts from one dimension to describe motion in a plane. This requires using vector mathematics to account for both magnitude and direction.

Position and Displacement

Definition: Position and Displacement

The position vector \(\vec{r}\) of an object in two dimensions specifies its location relative to a coordinate system. In Cartesian coordinates:

\(\vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j}\)

where \(x\) and \(y\) are the coordinates, and \(\hat{i}\) and \(\hat{j}\) are unit vectors in the \(x\) and \(y\) directions.

The displacement vector \(\Delta\vec{r}\) is the change in position:

\(\Delta\vec{r} = \vec{r}_f - \vec{r}_i = (x_f - x_i)\hat{i} + (y_f - y_i)\hat{j} = \Delta x\hat{i} + \Delta y\hat{j}\)
Figure 3.1: Position vectors \(\vec{r}_A\) and \(\vec{r}_B\) from the origin, and the displacement \(\Delta\vec{r} = \vec{r}_B - \vec{r}_A\) between them.
Example 3.1: Displacement from Coordinates

A hiker walks from a position \(A\) with coordinates \((1\,\text{km}, 2\,\text{km})\) to a camp \(B\) at \((4\,\text{km}, 6\,\text{km})\). The displacement vector is:

\(\Delta\vec{r} = (4 - 1)\hat{i} + (6 - 2)\hat{j} = 3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j}\,\text{km}\)

The magnitude of the displacement, which is the straight-line distance, is:

\(|\Delta\vec{r}| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5\,\text{km}\)
Key Concept: Position Versus Displacement

A position is where an object is, with a specified reference frame. A displacement is the change of position, which is itself a vector. The same displacement can occur between many different pairs of positions, and it is independent of how we labelled the points.

Velocity

Definition: Average and Instantaneous Velocity

The average velocity \(\vec{v}_{\text{avg}}\) in two dimensions is:

\(\vec{v}_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta\vec{r}}{\Delta t} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\hat{i} + \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t}\hat{j}\)

The instantaneous velocity \(\vec{v}\) is the derivative of the position vector:

\(\vec{v} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{\Delta\vec{r}}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} = \frac{dx}{dt}\hat{i} + \frac{dy}{dt}\hat{j} = v_x\hat{i} + v_y\hat{j}\)
Key Concept: Properties of Velocity in Two Dimensions
  • Velocity is a vector quantity with both magnitude and direction.
  • The magnitude of velocity is the speed: \(v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}\).
  • The direction of velocity is tangent to the path of motion.
  • The components \(v_x\) and \(v_y\) represent the rates of change in the \(x\) and \(y\) directions.
Figure 3.2: Velocity vectors are always tangent to the path of motion.
Practice 3.1: Average and Instantaneous Velocity

The position of a particle is given by \(\vec{r}(t) = (3t^2)\hat{i} + (4t - t^3)\hat{j}\) meters.

  1. Find the velocity vector at time \(t = 2\) seconds.
  2. Find the acceleration vector at time \(t = 2\) seconds.
Solution
  1. First, find the velocity function by differentiating the position function:
    \(\vec{v}(t) = \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(3t^2)\hat{i} + \frac{d}{dt}(4t - t^3)\hat{j} = (6t)\hat{i} + (4 - 3t^2)\hat{j}\)

    Now, evaluate at \(t = 2\) s:
    \(\vec{v}(2) = (6 \times 2)\hat{i} + (4 - 3 \times 2^2)\hat{j} = 12\hat{i} + (4 - 12)\hat{j} = 12\hat{i} - 8\hat{j}\)
    (The units are metres per second.)
  2. Next, find the acceleration function by differentiating the velocity function:
    \(\vec{a}(t) = \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(6t)\hat{i} + \frac{d}{dt}(4 - 3t^2)\hat{j} = 6\hat{i} - 6t\hat{j}\)

    Now, evaluate at \(t = 2\) s:
    \(\vec{a}(2) = 6\hat{i} - (6 \times 2)\hat{j} = 6\hat{i} - 12\hat{j}\)
    (The units are metres per second squared.)

Acceleration

Definition: Average and Instantaneous Acceleration

The average acceleration \(\vec{a}_{\text{avg}}\) is the change in velocity over time:

\(\vec{a}_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta\vec{v}}{\Delta t} = \frac{\vec{v}_f - \vec{v}_i}{\Delta t}\)

The instantaneous acceleration \(\vec{a}\) is the derivative of the velocity vector:

\(\vec{a} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{\Delta\vec{v}}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = \frac{dv_x}{dt}\hat{i} + \frac{dv_y}{dt}\hat{j} = a_x\hat{i} + a_y\hat{j}\)
Key Concept: Properties of Acceleration in Two Dimensions
  • Acceleration is a vector quantity with magnitude \(a = \sqrt{a_x^2 + a_y^2}\) and direction.
  • Acceleration can change the magnitude of velocity (speeding up or slowing down).
  • Acceleration can change the direction of velocity (turning).
  • The direction of acceleration is not necessarily the same as the direction of velocity.
Practice 3.2: Acceleration of a Particle

Given the position vector \(\vec{r}(t) = (3t^2)\hat{i} + (4t - t^3)\hat{j}\), find the velocity and acceleration vectors at time \(t = 2\) seconds, and use them to describe the motion.

Solution

We have already found in Practice 3.1 that \(\vec{v}(2) = 12\hat{i} - 8\hat{j}\,\text{m/s}\) and \(\vec{a}(2) = 6\hat{i} - 12\hat{j}\,\text{m/s}^2\). At \(t = 2\) s the particle is speeding up or slowing down depending on the dot product of \(\vec{v}\) and \(\vec{a}\).

\(\vec{v} \cdot \vec{a} = (12)(6) + (-8)(-12) = 72 + 96 = 168 > 0\)

The dot product is positive, so the acceleration has a component in the direction of velocity; the particle is speeding up. Its acceleration is not purely radial or tangential here because the motion is neither purely circular nor purely straight.

3.2 Projectile Motion

Projectile motion is a classic example of two-dimensional motion where an object moves under the influence of gravity only, with all other forces like air resistance neglected. The path followed by a projectile is its trajectory, which is parabolic.

Definition: Projectile Motion

Projectile motion is the motion of an object thrown or projected into the air, subject only to the acceleration of gravity. The path followed by a projectile is its trajectory, which is parabolic.

Key Concept: Characteristics of Projectile Motion
  • The motion is separated into independent horizontal and vertical components.
  • Horizontal motion: The acceleration is zero (\(a_x = 0\)). The horizontal velocity is constant (\(v_x = v_{0x}\)).
  • Vertical motion: The acceleration is constant and downwards (\(a_y = -g \approx -9.8\,\text{m/s}^2\)). The vertical velocity changes uniformly.

Kinematic Equations for Projectile Motion

For a projectile launched with initial velocity \(\vec{v}_0\) at an angle \(\theta\) to the horizontal, the initial velocity components are \(v_{0x} = v_0\cos\theta\) and \(v_{0y} = v_0\sin\theta\).

Horizontal motion:

\(\begin{aligned} a_x &= 0 \\ v_x &= v_{0x} \\ x &= x_0 + v_{0x}t \end{aligned}\)

Vertical motion:

\(\begin{aligned} a_y &= -g \\ v_y &= v_{0y} - gt \\ y &= y_0 + v_{0y}t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \\ v_y^2 &= v_{0y}^2 - 2g\Delta y \end{aligned}\)
Useful Equations (for launch and landing at same height)
  • Time of flight: \(T = \frac{2v_0\sin\theta}{g}\)
  • Maximum height: \(H = \frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}\)
  • Range: \(R = \frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}\)

The Trajectory Equation

The shape of a projectile's path can be found by eliminating time. From the horizontal equation, \(t = x/v_{0x}\). Substituting into the vertical equation gives

\(y = (\tan\theta)x - \frac{g}{2v_0^2\cos^2\theta}x^2\)

This is the equation of a downward-opening parabola. It shows explicitly that the path is not a straight line even though the horizontal motion is uniform: gravity bends the trajectory increasingly as \(x\) grows.

The range formula follows by setting \(y = 0\) and solving for \(x\). Because \(\sin 2\theta\) is symmetric about \(45^\circ\), two launch angles that add to \(90^\circ\) (such as \(30^\circ\) and \(60^\circ\)) produce the same range, although the higher-angle shot stays in the air longer and reaches a greater height. For a given launch speed, the maximum range occurs at exactly \(45^\circ\) when air resistance is neglected.

Example 3.2: Height at a Given Horizontal Distance

A projectile is launched at \(20\,\text{m/s}\) and \(30^\circ\) above the horizontal. Find its height when it has traveled \(15\,\text{m}\) horizontally.

Solution

The horizontal velocity is \(v_{0x} = 20\cos 30^\circ = 17.3\,\text{m/s}\), so the time to travel \(15\,\text{m}\) is

\(t = \frac{x}{v_{0x}} = \frac{15}{17.3} = 0.866\,\text{s}\)

The height is then

\(y = (20\sin 30^\circ)(0.866) - \tfrac{1}{2}(9.8)(0.866)^2 = 8.66 - 3.67 \approx 5.0\,\text{m}\)

Equivalently, substituting directly into the trajectory equation gives the same result.

Figure 3.3: The parabolic trajectory of a projectile, showing velocity components and constant downward acceleration.
Practice 3.2: Dropped from a Cliff

A ball is thrown from a cliff \(80\,\text{m}\) high with an initial horizontal speed of \(15\,\text{m/s}\).

  1. How long does it take to reach the ground?
  2. How far from the base of the cliff does it land?
Solution

Given: \(y_0 = 80\,\text{m}\), \(y = 0\), \(v_{0x} = 15\,\text{m/s}\), \(v_{0y} = 0\).

  1. We use the vertical motion equation to find the time:
    \(y = y_0 + v_{0y}t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2\)
    \(0 = 80 + (0)t - \frac{1}{2}(9.8)t^2\)
    \(4.9t^2 = 80\)
    \(t = \sqrt{\frac{80}{4.9}} \approx 4.04\,\text{s}\)
  2. Now we use the horizontal motion equation to find the range:
    \(x = x_0 + v_{0x}t\)
    \(x = 0 + (15)(4.04) \approx 60.6\,\text{m}\)

3.3 Circular Motion

Circular motion describes an object moving along a circular path.

Uniform Circular Motion

Definition: Uniform Circular Motion

Uniform circular motion is the motion of an object traveling at a constant speed along a circular path. Even though the speed is constant, the velocity is continuously changing because its direction is changing. This change in velocity means there must be an acceleration.

Key Concept: Centripetal Acceleration

In uniform circular motion, the acceleration is directed toward the center of the circle. This is called centripetal acceleration (\(a_c\)). Its magnitude is given by: \(a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}\), where \(v\) is the constant speed and \(r\) is the radius of the circle. The time for one complete revolution is the period (\(T\)), given by \(T = \frac{2\pi r}{v}\).

To see where the centripetal acceleration comes from, consider two nearby positions on the circle separated by a small angle \(\Delta\theta\). The velocity vectors at those positions have the same length \(v\) but different directions, and the change in velocity \(\Delta\vec{v}\) points toward the center. The triangle formed by the two velocity vectors is similar to the triangle formed by the two position vectors, so \(\frac{|\Delta\vec{v}|}{v} \approx \frac{|\Delta\vec{r}|}{r}\). For a small interval, \(|\Delta\vec{r}| \approx v\Delta t\), and therefore \(a_c = \lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{|\Delta\vec{v}|}{\Delta t} = \frac{v}{r}\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{|\Delta\vec{r}|}{\Delta t} = \frac{v^2}{r}\). This derivation explains why the acceleration is directed toward the center: it is produced by the continuous change in the direction of the velocity, not by a change in speed.

Figure 3.4: In uniform circular motion, the velocity vector \(\vec{v}\) is tangent to the circle, while the centripetal acceleration \(\vec{a}_c\) points to the center.

Tangential and Radial Acceleration

Definition: Radial and Tangential Acceleration
  • Radial Acceleration (\(a_r\)): This is the centripetal acceleration, responsible for changing the direction of the velocity. It always points toward the center. \(a_r = a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}\)
  • Tangential Acceleration (\(a_t\)): This component is tangent to the circular path and is responsible for changing the speed of the object. \(a_t = \frac{dv}{dt}\)

The total acceleration \(\vec{a}\) is the vector sum: \(\vec{a} = \vec{a}_r + \vec{a}_t\). The magnitude is \(a = \sqrt{a_r^2 + a_t^2}\).

3.4 Relative Motion in Two Dimensions

Definition: Relative Motion

Relative motion analysis is used when objects are moving with respect to a reference frame that is also moving. The key equation is:

\(\vec{v}_{A/C} = \vec{v}_{A/B} + \vec{v}_{B/C}\)

where \(\vec{v}_{A/C}\) is the velocity of object \(A\) relative to frame \(C\), \(\vec{v}_{A/B}\) is the velocity of \(A\) relative to frame \(B\), and \(\vec{v}_{B/C}\) is the velocity of frame \(B\) relative to frame \(C\).

Example 3.3: Boat and River

A boat that can travel at \(4\,\text{m/s}\) in still water heads directly across a river that is flowing east at \(3\,\text{m/s}\). What is the velocity of the boat relative to the ground?

Solution

Let \(B =\) Boat, \(W =\) Water, \(G =\) Ground. We are given:

  • \(\vec{v}_{B/W} = 4\hat{j}\,\text{m/s}\) (velocity of boat relative to water)
  • \(\vec{v}_{W/G} = 3\hat{i}\,\text{m/s}\) (velocity of water relative to ground)

We want to find \(\vec{v}_{B/G}\). Using the relative velocity formula:

\(\vec{v}_{B/G} = \vec{v}_{B/W} + \vec{v}_{W/G} = 3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j}\,\text{m/s}\)

The speed relative to the ground is \(|\vec{v}_{B/G}| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5\,\text{m/s}\). The direction is \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(4/3) \approx 53.1^\circ\) north of east.

Example 3.4: Heading Upstream

A river flows east at \(3.0\,\text{m/s}\). A boat can travel at \(5.0\,\text{m/s}\) in still water. At what angle upstream should the boat head so that it lands directly across the river, and how long does the crossing take if the river is \(100\,\text{m}\) wide?

Solution

Let the boat's heading make an angle \(\theta\) upstream of the north direction. The eastward component of the boat's velocity relative to the water must cancel the river current:

\(5.0\sin\theta = 3.0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \theta = \sin^{-1}(0.60) \approx 36.9^\circ\)

The northward component is then

\(v_{\text{north}} = 5.0\cos 36.9^\circ = 4.0\,\text{m/s}\)

The crossing time is

\(t = \frac{100\,\text{m}}{4.0\,\text{m/s}} = 25\,\text{s}\)

Pointing upstream is essential; if the boat headed straight across, the current would carry it downstream while it crossed.

Problem-Solving Workshop

Projectile problems look like two separate one-dimensional problems connected only by time. The horizontal motion has no acceleration, while the vertical motion has the constant acceleration \(-g\). Keeping those two columns separate prevents the most common mistake: applying a single acceleration to the whole velocity vector.

Resolve the launch velocity first, then choose axes with \(+x\) in the direction of horizontal travel and \(+y\) upward. Write the known components, decide whether the unknown is a time, a height, or a range, and select the equation that contains that quantity. At the end, reconstruct the final velocity from its components rather than guessing its direction.

Problem-Solving Checklist
  1. Draw the trajectory and mark the launch point, landing point, and positive axes.
  2. Resolve the initial velocity into \(v_{0x} = v_0\cos\theta\) and \(v_{0y} = v_0\sin\theta\).
  3. Set \(a_x = 0\) and \(a_y = -g\); never mix horizontal and vertical equations.
  4. Use the vertical motion to find the time of flight or the maximum height.
  5. Use \(x = v_{0x}t\) to find the horizontal range.
  6. Combine \(v_x\) and \(v_y\) at the end to obtain the final speed and direction.
Worked Example

A ball is kicked from level ground with an initial speed of \(25\,\text{m/s}\) at \(30^\circ\) above the horizontal. Neglecting air resistance, find the time of flight, the maximum height, the horizontal range, and the velocity components just before the ball lands.

Solution

Take the launch point as the origin, with \(+x\) horizontal and \(+y\) upward. The initial components are:

\(v_{0x} = 25\cos 30^\circ = 21.7\,\text{m/s}, \qquad v_{0y} = 25\sin 30^\circ = 12.5\,\text{m/s}\)

Time of flight. Because the ground is level, the vertical displacement is zero. Using \(y = v_{0y}t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2\) with \(g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2\):

\(0 = t\left(12.5 - 4.9t\right) \quad \Rightarrow \quad t = \frac{12.5}{4.9} = 2.55\,\text{s}\)

Maximum height. At the top \(v_y = 0\), so:

\(H = \frac{v_{0y}^2}{2g} = \frac{(12.5)^2}{2(9.8)} = \frac{156.25}{19.6} = 7.97\,\text{m}\)

Range. The horizontal velocity is constant:

\(R = v_{0x}t = (21.7)(2.55) = 55.3\,\text{m}\)

Velocity at landing. The horizontal component is unchanged, and the vertical component is reversed by symmetry:

\(v_x = 21.7\,\text{m/s}, \qquad v_y = -12.5\,\text{m/s}\)

The speed is \(\sqrt{(21.7)^2 + (12.5)^2} = 25.0\,\text{m/s}\), directed \(30^\circ\) below the horizontal.

Why This Matters

Sports scientists and coaches use these equations when analysing a football kick. A ball struck at \(25\,\text{m/s}\) and \(30^\circ\) would stay airborne for about \(2.6\,\text{s}\) and land roughly \(55\,\text{m}\) away if air resistance were negligible; in a real match, drag shortens the flight, so players learn to strike harder or at a slightly different angle. The same component method is used in video analysis to separate technique from luck.

Key Takeaways

  • Horizontal and vertical motions are independent and are linked only by the common time.
  • With air resistance neglected, \(v_x\) stays constant while \(v_y\) changes at the rate \(-g\).
  • Resolving the launch velocity into components is the essential first step.
  • The time of flight on level ground is \(T = 2v_0\sin\theta/g\).
  • The maximum height depends only on the vertical component of the launch velocity.
  • For level ground and no drag, the range is \(R = v_0^2\sin 2\theta/g\), which peaks at \(\theta = 45^\circ\).
  • The landing speed equals the launch speed on level ground, but the velocity direction is reversed in the vertical component.
