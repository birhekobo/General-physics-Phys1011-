Chapter 11: Electromagnetism and Electronics

Welcome to Electromagnetism and Electronics

Electromagnetism governs how electric and magnetic fields are produced and interact, and it underpins the electronics that power modern technology. Coulomb's law and electric fields describe the forces between charges, while electric potential gives energy per charge and leads to electric potential energy.

Current, resistance, and Ohm's law form the foundation of electrical circuits, and electrical power measures the rate of energy transfer. Moving into magnetic fields, flux, induction, and electromagnetic waves completes the classical picture of how electricity and magnetism are unified in Maxwell's equations.

11.1 Coulomb's Law and Electric Fields

11.1.1 Coulomb's Law

Definition

Coulomb's Law states that the magnitude of the electrostatic force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them:

\(F = k \frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}\)

where \(k = 8.99 \times 10^9\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}^2\) is Coulomb's constant.

The force is attractive if the charges have opposite signs and repulsive if they have the same sign.

Example

Two point charges, \(q_1 = +2.0\,\mu\text{C}\) and \(q_2 = -3.0\,\mu\text{C}\), are separated by 0.5 m. Calculate the electrostatic force between them.

Solution
\(F = k \frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2} = (8.99 \times 10^9) \frac{|(2.0 \times 10^{-6})(-3.0 \times 10^{-6})|}{(0.5)^2}\)
\(= (8.99 \times 10^9) \frac{6.0 \times 10^{-12}}{0.25} = (8.99 \times 10^9)(2.4 \times 10^{-11}) = 0.2158\,\text{N}\)

The force is attractive since the charges have opposite signs.

11.1.2 Electric Fields

Definition

An electric field is a region of space around a charged object where a test charge would experience an electric force. The electric field \(\vec{E}\) at a point is defined as:

\(\vec{E} = \frac{\vec{F}}{q_0}\)

where \(\vec{F}\) is the force experienced by a test charge \(q_0\) placed at that point.

For a point charge \(q\), the electric field at distance \(r\) is:

\(E = k \frac{|q|}{r^2}\)
Figure 11.2: Electric field lines of an electric dipole
Important

Electric field lines:

  • Begin on positive charges and end on negative charges
  • Never cross each other
  • Are denser where the field is stronger
  • The direction of the field is tangent to the field lines
Example

Calculate the electric field 0.3 m from a point charge of \(+5.0\,\mu\text{C}\).

Solution
\(E = k \frac{|q|}{r^2} = (8.99 \times 10^9) \frac{5.0 \times 10^{-6}}{(0.3)^2} = (8.99 \times 10^9) \frac{5.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.09}\)
\(= (8.99 \times 10^9)(5.56 \times 10^{-5}) = 5.0 \times 10^5\,\text{N/C}\)

The field points radially outward from the positive charge.

Key Concept

For continuous charge distributions, we integrate the contributions of infinitesimal charge elements:

\(\vec{E} = \int d\vec{E} = \int k \frac{dq}{r^2} \hat{r}\)

This approach is used for lines of charge, charged surfaces, and charged volumes.

11.2 Electric Potential and Electric Potential Energy of Point Charge

Definition

Electric potential energy is the energy a charged object possesses due to its position in an electric field. For two point charges:

\(U = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{r}\)
Definition

Electric potential (voltage) at a point is the electric potential energy per unit charge:

\(V = \frac{U}{q_0}\)

For a point charge:

\(V = k \frac{q}{r}\)
Important

Electric potential is a scalar quantity, while electric field is a vector quantity. Potential differences are measured in volts (V).

Example

Calculate the electric potential 0.2 m from a point charge of \(+3.0\,\mu\text{C}\).

Solution
\(V = k \frac{q}{r} = (8.99 \times 10^9) \frac{3.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.2} = (8.99 \times 10^9)(1.5 \times 10^{-5}) = 1.35 \times 10^5\,\text{V}\)
Example

Two point charges, \(q_1 = +2.0\,\mu\text{C}\) and \(q_2 = -2.0\,\mu\text{C}\), are \(0.60\,\text{m}\) apart. Find the electric potential at the midpoint between them.

Solution

Potential is a scalar, so the contributions simply add algebraically. Each charge is \(0.30\,\text{m}\) from the midpoint:

\(V = k\frac{q_1}{r_1} + k\frac{q_2}{r_2} = (8.99\times10^9)\left(\frac{2.0\times10^{-6}}{0.30} + \frac{-2.0\times10^{-6}}{0.30}\right) = 0\)

The field at the midpoint is not zero, however; it points from the positive toward the negative charge. This illustrates that potential and field are different quantities.

11.3 Current, Resistance and Ohm's Law

11.3.1 Current

Definition

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge:

\(I = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}\)

where \(I\) is current in amperes (A), \(\Delta Q\) is charge in coulombs (C), and \(\Delta t\) is time in seconds (s).

11.3.2 Resistance and Ohm's Law

Definition

Resistance is the opposition to the flow of electric current. For a given material, the resistance is:

\(R = \rho \frac{L}{A}\)

where \(\rho\) is the resistivity, \(L\) is the length, and \(A\) is the cross-sectional area.

Definition

Ohm's Law states that the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it and inversely proportional to its resistance:

\(V = IR\)
Example

A copper wire (\(\rho = 1.68 \times 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}\)) has length 10 m and diameter 2 mm. Calculate its resistance.

Solution

First, find the cross-sectional area:

\(A = \pi r^2 = \pi (0.001)^2 = 3.14 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2\)

Now calculate resistance:

\(R = \rho \frac{L}{A} = (1.68 \times 10^{-8}) \frac{10}{3.14 \times 10^{-6}} = (1.68 \times 10^{-8})(3.18 \times 10^6) = 0.0534\,\Omega\)
Important

The resistivity of materials depends on temperature:

\(\rho = \rho_0 [1 + \alpha (T - T_0)]\)

where \(\alpha\) is the temperature coefficient of resistivity.

Example

A 12 V battery is connected to a resistor with a resistance of 4.5 ohms. Find the current through the circuit and the electrical power dissipated.

Solution

Using Ohm's law, \(I = V/R\), the current is

\(I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{12}{4.5} = 2.67\,\text{A}\)

The electrical power dissipated is

\(P = VI = (12)(2.67) = 32\,\text{W}\)

11.4 Electrical Energy and Power

Definition

Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred:

\(P = IV = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}\)
Definition

Electrical energy consumed over time:

\(E = Pt = IVt\)
Example

A 60 W light bulb operates on 120 V. Calculate:

a) The current through the bulb
b) The resistance of the bulb
c) The energy consumed in 5 hours
d) The cost of operating the bulb for 5 hours if electricity costs $0.12 per kWh

Solution

a) \(P = IV \Rightarrow I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{60}{120} = 0.5\,\text{A}\)

b) \(R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{120}{0.5} = 240\,\Omega\) or \(R = \frac{V^2}{P} = \frac{120^2}{60} = 240\,\Omega\)

c) \(E = Pt = 60\,\text{W} \times 5\,\text{h} = 300\,\text{Wh} = 0.3\,\text{kWh}\)

d) Cost = \(0.3\,\text{kWh} \times \$0.12/\text{kWh} = \$0.036\)

Practice Problem

An electric heater has a resistance of 20 Ω and operates on 240 V. Calculate:

a) The power consumption
b) The current drawn
c) The energy consumed in 8 hours of operation
d) The cost of operation if electricity costs $0.15 per kWh

11.5 Equivalent Resistance and Kirchhoff's Law

11.5.1 Sources of Electromotive Forces (emf)

Definition

Electromotive force (emf) is the energy per unit charge supplied by a source such as a battery or generator. It represents the maximum potential difference between the terminals of the source.

Important

Real voltage sources have internal resistance \(r\), so the terminal voltage is:

\(V = \mathcal{E} - Ir\)

where \(\mathcal{E}\) is the emf and \(I\) is the current.

11.5.2 Combinations of Resistors

Key Concept

Series combination:

\(R_{\text{eq}} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 + \cdots\)

The same current flows through all resistors.

Key Concept

Parallel combination:

\(\frac{1}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3} + \cdots\)

The same voltage appears across all resistors.

Figure 11.3: Series and parallel combinations of resistors
Example

Calculate the equivalent resistance of three resistors: \(R_1 = 10\,\Omega\), \(R_2 = 20\,\Omega\), and \(R_3 = 30\,\Omega\) connected in:

a) Series
b) Parallel

Solution

a) Series: \(R_{\text{eq}} = 10 + 20 + 30 = 60\,\Omega\)

b) Parallel:

\(\frac{1}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{20} + \frac{1}{30} = \frac{6 + 3 + 2}{60} = \frac{11}{60}\)
\(R_{\text{eq}} = \frac{60}{11} \approx 5.45\,\Omega\)

11.5.3 Kirchhoff's Law

Definition

Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL): The sum of currents entering a junction equals the sum of currents leaving the junction.

\(\sum I_{\text{in}} = \sum I_{\text{out}}\)
Definition

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL): The sum of potential differences around any closed loop in a circuit is zero.

\(\sum V = 0\)
Circuit Example

Analyze the circuit below using Kirchhoff's laws:

Figure 11.4: Circuit for the Kirchhoff's laws example
Solution

Let's find the equivalent resistance:

  • The 6 Ω and 2 Ω resistors are in parallel:
    \(\frac{1}{R_{6,2}} = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{3}{6} = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3} \;\Rightarrow\; R_{6,2} = 1.5\,\Omega\)
  • This combination is in series with the 4 Ω and 8 Ω resistors:
    \(R_{\text{eq}} = 4 + 1.5 + 8 = 13.5\,\Omega\)
  • Total current: \(I = \frac{V}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{12}{13.5} \approx 0.889\) A

11.6 Magnetic Field and Magnetic Flux

11.6.1 Magnetic Field

Definition

A magnetic field is a vector field that describes the magnetic influence on moving electric charges, electric currents, and magnetic materials. The SI unit is tesla (T).

Figure 11.6: Magnetic field lines around a bar magnet
Important

Magnetic field lines:

  • Emerge from the north pole and enter the south pole
  • Form closed loops (no magnetic monopoles)
  • Never cross each other
  • Are denser where the field is stronger

The magnetic force on a moving charge:

\(\vec{F} = q(\vec{v} \times \vec{B})\)

where \(q\) is the charge, \(\vec{v}\) is the velocity, and \(\vec{B}\) is the magnetic field.

The magnetic force on a current-carrying wire:

\(\vec{F} = I(\vec{L} \times \vec{B})\)

where \(I\) is the current and \(\vec{L}\) is the length vector of the wire.

Example

A proton moves with speed \(2.0 \times 10^6\) m/s perpendicular to a magnetic field of 0.5 T. Calculate:

a) The magnetic force on the proton
b) The radius of its circular path

Solution

a) \(F = qvB\sin\theta = (1.6 \times 10^{-19})(2.0 \times 10^6)(0.5)\sin 90^\circ = 1.6 \times 10^{-13}\,\text{N}\)

b) The magnetic force provides centripetal force:

\(qvB = \frac{mv^2}{r} \;\Rightarrow\; r = \frac{mv}{qB} = \frac{(1.67 \times 10^{-27})(2.0 \times 10^6)}{(1.6 \times 10^{-19})(0.5)} = 0.04175\,\text{m}\)

11.6.2 Magnetic Flux

Definition

Magnetic flux is a measure of the total magnetic field passing through a surface:

\(\Phi_B = \vec{B} \cdot \vec{A} = BA\cos\theta\)

where \(\theta\) is the angle between the magnetic field and the normal to the surface.

Figure 11.7: Magnetic flux through a tilted loop

The SI unit of magnetic flux is the weber (Wb), where 1 Wb = 1 T·m².

Example

A rectangular loop with area 0.2 m² is placed in a uniform magnetic field of 0.5 T. Calculate the magnetic flux when:

a) The field is perpendicular to the plane of the loop
b) The field makes a 30° angle with the normal to the loop

Solution

a) \(\theta = 0^\circ\), \(\Phi_B = BA\cos\theta = (0.5)(0.2)\cos 0^\circ = 0.1\) Wb

b) \(\theta = 30^\circ\), \(\Phi_B = BA\cos\theta = (0.5)(0.2)\cos 30^\circ = 0.1 \times 0.866 = 0.0866\) Wb

Example

A straight wire of length \(0.50\,\text{m}\) carries a current of \(4.0\,\text{A}\) perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of \(0.20\,\text{T}\). Find the magnitude of the magnetic force on the wire.

Solution

For a wire perpendicular to the field:

\(F = ILB = (4.0)(0.50)(0.20) = 0.40\,\text{N}\)

The direction is given by the right-hand rule: point the fingers of the right hand along the current and curl them toward the field; the thumb points along the force. If the wire were parallel to the field, the force would be zero.

11.7 Electromagnetic Induction

Definition

Faraday's Law of Induction states that the induced electromotive force in any closed circuit is equal to the negative of the time rate of change of the magnetic flux through the circuit:

\(\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}\)
Definition

Lenz's Law states that the direction of the induced current is such that it opposes the change in magnetic flux that produced it.

Figure 11.8: Electromagnetic induction in a coil
Example

A coil with 100 turns experiences a change in magnetic flux from 0.02 Wb to 0.08 Wb in 0.5 seconds. Calculate the induced emf.

Solution
\(\mathcal{E} = -N \frac{\Delta \Phi_B}{\Delta t} = -100 \frac{0.08 - 0.02}{0.5} = -100 \frac{0.06}{0.5} = -100 \times 0.12 = -12\,\text{V}\)

The negative sign indicates the direction of the induced emf opposes the change in flux.

a) The induced emf
b) The induced current
c) The direction of the current

11.7.1 Transformers

A transformer uses mutual induction to change an alternating voltage. Two coils share a common iron core: an alternating current in the primary coil produces a changing magnetic flux, which induces an emf in the secondary coil. For an ideal transformer:

\(\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p}, \qquad V_p I_p = V_s I_s\)

where \(N_p\) and \(N_s\) are the numbers of turns. A step-up transformer has \(N_s > N_p\) and raises the voltage; a step-down transformer lowers it. Power is conserved in the ideal case, so a voltage increase is accompanied by a current decrease.

Example

A transformer has \(400\) turns in its primary and \(100\) turns in its secondary. The primary is connected to \(240\,\text{V}\) AC and draws \(0.50\,\text{A}\). Find the secondary voltage and current.

Solution
\(V_s = V_p\frac{N_s}{N_p} = 240\frac{100}{400} = 60\,\text{V}\)

Assuming an ideal transformer, \(V_pI_p = V_sI_s\), so:

\(I_s = \frac{V_pI_p}{V_s} = \frac{(240)(0.50)}{60} = 2.0\,\text{A}\)

This is a step-down transformer: the voltage falls by a factor of four while the current rises by the same factor.

11.8 Semiconductors and Diodes

11.8.1 Intrinsic Semiconductors

Definition

Intrinsic semiconductors are pure semiconductor materials without significant dopants. Silicon and germanium are common examples.

At absolute zero temperature, intrinsic semiconductors behave as insulators. At higher temperatures, electron-hole pairs are created, allowing conduction.

11.8.2 Extrinsic Semiconductors

Definition

Extrinsic semiconductors are semiconductors doped with impurities to enhance their conductivity.

  • n-type semiconductors: Doped with donor atoms (e.g., phosphorus in silicon) that provide extra electrons
  • p-type semiconductors: Doped with acceptor atoms (e.g., boron in silicon) that create holes

11.8.3 The pn-junction

Definition

A pn-junction is formed when p-type and n-type semiconductors are joined together. This creates a diode, which allows current to flow easily in one direction but restricts it in the opposite direction.

Figure 11.9: Structure of a pn-junction
Important

The pn-junction exhibits:

  • Forward bias: Positive voltage applied to p-side, negative to n-side. Current flows easily.
  • Reverse bias: Positive voltage applied to n-side, negative to p-side. Very little current flows.

11.8.4 Active Circuit Elements

Definition

Active circuit elements can control electron flow and provide gain. Diodes and transistors are examples.

Circuit Example

Analyze a simple diode circuit. Assume the diode has a forward voltage drop of 0.7 V.

Figure 11.10: Simple forward-biased diode circuit
Solution

The diode is forward biased. The voltage across the resistor is:

\(V_R = 5 - 0.7 = 4.3\,\text{V}\)

The current through the circuit is:

\(I = \frac{V_R}{R} = \frac{4.3}{1000} = 4.3\,\text{mA}\)
Practice Problem

Design a circuit using a diode and resistor that will limit the voltage across a load to 5.1 V when the input voltage can vary from 0 to 12 V. Specify the required components and their values.

11.9 Capacitors and RC Circuits

Definition

A capacitor is a device that stores electrical energy in an electric field. It consists of two conducting plates separated by an insulator.

The capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor is:

\(C = \frac{\epsilon_0 A}{d}\)

where \(\epsilon_0\) is the permittivity of free space, \(A\) is the area of one plate, and \(d\) is the separation between plates.

Figure 11.11: Parallel-plate capacitor
Example

A parallel-plate capacitor has plates of area 0.1 m² separated by 1 mm. Calculate its capacitance.

Solution
\(C = \frac{\epsilon_0 A}{d} = \frac{(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(0.1)}{0.001} = 8.85 \times 10^{-10}\,\text{F} = 885\,\text{pF}\)

The charge stored by a capacitor is \(Q = CV\), and the energy stored in its electric field is:

\(U = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{1}{2}QV = \frac{Q^2}{2C}\)

The energy is proportional to the square of the voltage, so doubling the voltage quadruples the stored energy.

Example

A \(100\,\mu\text{F}\) capacitor is connected to a \(12\,\text{V}\) battery. Find the charge and the energy stored.

Solution
\(Q = CV = (100\times10^{-6})(12) = 1.2\times10^{-3}\,\text{C} = 1.2\,\text{mC}\)
\(U = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{1}{2}(100\times10^{-6})(12)^2 = 7.2\times10^{-3}\,\text{J} = 7.2\,\text{mJ}\)
Key Concept

In RC circuits, the charging and discharging of capacitors follows exponential behavior:

\(V_C(t) = V_0 (1 - e^{-t/RC}) \quad \text{(charging)}\)
\(V_C(t) = V_0 e^{-t/RC} \quad \text{(discharging)}\)

where \(RC\) is the time constant.

Example

A 220 \(\mu\text{F}\) capacitor is charged to 12 V through a 1.0 k\(\Omega\) resistor. Find the time constant and the voltage across the capacitor after one time constant.

Solution

The time constant is

\(\tau = RC = (1.0 \times 10^3)(220 \times 10^{-6}) = 2.2 \times 10^{-1}\,\text{s}\)

For a discharging capacitor, after one time constant the voltage has fallen to \(e^{-1} \approx 36.8\%\) of its initial value:

\(V_C = V_0 e^{-t/RC} = 12\,e^{-0.22/0.22} = 12e^{-1} \approx 4.42\,\text{V}\)

So after one time constant the capacitor holds about 4.4 V.

11.10 Electromagnetic Waves

Definition

Electromagnetic waves are waves that consist of oscillating electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation.

Important

Properties of electromagnetic waves:

  • They travel at the speed of light in vacuum: \(c = 3.00 \times 10^8\) m/s
  • They obey the wave equation: \(c = f\lambda\)
  • They transport energy and momentum
  • They can be polarized
Figure 11.12: Structure of an electromagnetic wave
Example

Calculate the wavelength of an electromagnetic wave with frequency 100 MHz.

Solution
\(\lambda = \frac{c}{f} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^8}{100 \times 10^6} = 3\,\text{m}\)

This is in the radio wave part of the spectrum.

Problem-Solving Workshop

Circuit problems are best solved by redrawing the circuit rather than by searching for a formula. Label the source voltage, every resistance and capacitance, and the assumed direction of each current. Then simplify sections that are clearly in series or parallel, replacing each group by its equivalent resistance. Series elements carry the same current, while parallel branches share the same voltage, so these two facts let you move steadily from a complicated network to a single equivalent resistance.

RC circuits add a time dimension. Separate the initial state, the transient interval, and the long-time steady state. The product \(\tau=RC\) has units of seconds and sets the pace of charging or discharging: after one time constant a discharging capacitor has fallen to about 37% of its initial voltage, and a charging capacitor has reached about 63% of its final value. Use the exponential expressions rather than linear interpolation, and check that your answer approaches the expected initial and final values.

Problem-Solving Checklist
  1. Redraw the circuit and label the source, resistors, capacitors, and assumed current directions.
  2. Reduce series and parallel combinations step by step, checking that each replacement preserves current or voltage.
  3. Apply Ohm's law and the power relations \(P=IV=I^2R=V^2/R\) to the simplified circuit.
  4. Use Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws when the circuit cannot be reduced by series-parallel rules alone.
  5. For capacitors, find the equivalent capacitance and the time constant \(\tau=RC\).
  6. For RC transients, apply the exponential charging or discharging equation and check the initial and long-time limits.
Worked Example

A \(100\,\mu\text{F}\) capacitor is charged to \(12\,\text{V}\) and then discharged through a \(10\,\text{k}\Omega\) resistor. (a) Find the time constant and the initial discharge current. (b) Find the capacitor voltage after \(2.0\,\text{s}\). (c) How long does it take for the voltage to fall to \(3.0\,\text{V}\)?

Solution

Convert the capacitance and resistance to SI units:

\(C = 100\,\mu\text{F} = 1.00 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{F}, \qquad R = 10\,\text{k}\Omega = 1.00 \times 10^{4}\,\Omega\)

(a) The time constant is:

\(\tau = RC = (1.00 \times 10^{4}\,\Omega)(1.00 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{F}) = 1.00\,\text{s}\)

At the instant the discharge begins, the full capacitor voltage appears across the resistor, so Ohm's law gives:

\(I_0 = \frac{V_0}{R} = \frac{12\,\text{V}}{1.00 \times 10^{4}\,\Omega} = 1.2 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{A} = 1.2\,\text{mA}\)

(b) For discharging, \(V_C(t)=V_0 e^{-t/\tau}\). After \(2.0\,\text{s}\):

\(V_C = 12\,e^{-2.0/1.00} = 12(0.1353) \approx 1.62\,\text{V}\)

(c) Setting \(V_C=3.0\,\text{V}\):

\(3.0 = 12\,e^{-t/1.00} \;\Rightarrow\; e^{-t/1.00}=0.25 \;\Rightarrow\; t = -\ln(0.25) \approx 1.39\,\text{s}\)

The voltage reaches \(3.0\,\text{V}\) after about \(1.4\,\text{s}\).

Why This Matters

A camera flash stores energy in a capacitor and then releases it quickly through a lamp. The charging resistor controls how long the flash takes to recycle: a larger resistance or capacitance gives a longer time constant and a slower recharge. The same RC timing idea appears in windshield-wiper delays, audio filters, and the gentle pacing pulses of some medical devices.

Key Takeaways

  • Electric current is the rate of charge flow, and Ohm's law relates voltage, current, and resistance through \(V=IR\).
  • Electrical power can be written as \(P=IV=I^2R=V^2/R\), and energy is power multiplied by time.
  • Series resistors add directly and carry the same current; parallel resistors combine reciprocally and share the same voltage.
  • Kirchhoff's laws express charge conservation at junctions and energy conservation around closed loops.
  • A capacitor stores charge according to \(C=Q/V\), and its capacitance depends on plate area, separation, and dielectric material.
  • An RC circuit responds exponentially with time constant \(\tau=RC\), reaching about 63% of the final charge in one time constant.
  • Changing magnetic flux induces an emf, and oscillating electric and magnetic fields propagate as electromagnetic waves.

  2. Explain why birds can sit on high-voltage power lines without being electrocuted.
  3. What is the difference between electric potential and electric potential energy?
  4. Why does the resistance of a semiconductor decrease with increasing temperature, while that of a metal increases?
  5. Explain how a generator produces electricity.
  6. Why do capacitors block DC current but allow AC current to pass?
  7. What is the physical significance of the time constant in an RC circuit?
  8. How does the electromagnetic spectrum relate to everyday technologies?
  9. Explain why a diode allows current to flow in only one direction.
  10. What is the role of doping in semiconductors?
  11. Two charges produce zero electric potential at a point, yet the electric field there is not zero. How is this possible?
  12. Why is the equivalent resistance of a parallel combination always less than the smallest individual resistance?
  13. A capacitor stores energy but does not dissipate it as heat. Where is the energy stored?
  14. Explain why a transformer cannot increase the power delivered to a load.
  15. Why is the magnetic force on a charged particle always perpendicular to its velocity, and what consequence does this have for the particle's speed?
  16. Why do power lines transmit electricity at very high voltage but relatively low current?

Problems

  1. Two point charges, \(q_1 = +4.0\,\mu\text{C}\) and \(q_2 = -6.0\,\mu\text{C}\), are separated by 0.8 m. Calculate:
    1. The electrostatic force between them
    2. The electric field midway between them
    3. The electric potential midway between them
  2. A circuit contains three resistors: \(R_1 = 12\,\Omega\), \(R_2 = 18\,\Omega\), and \(R_3 = 24\,\Omega\) connected in parallel to a 24 V battery. Calculate:
    1. The equivalent resistance
    2. The total current
    3. The current through each resistor
    4. The power dissipated in each resistor
  3. A proton moves with speed \(2.0 \times 10^6\) m/s perpendicular to a magnetic field of 0.5 T. Calculate:
    1. The magnetic force on the proton
    2. The radius of its circular path
    3. The period of revolution
    (Proton mass = \(1.67 \times 10^{-27}\) kg, charge = \(1.60 \times 10^{-19}\) C)
  4. A transformer has 200 turns in its primary coil and 50 turns in its secondary coil. If the primary voltage is 120 V AC, what is the secondary voltage? If the primary current is 2 A, what is the secondary current (assuming ideal transformer)?
  5. A silicon diode has a forward voltage drop of 0.7 V. If it's connected in series with a \(470\,\Omega\) resistor to a 9 V battery, calculate:
    1. The current in the circuit
    2. The power dissipated in the resistor
    3. The power dissipated in the diode
  6. A parallel-plate capacitor has plates of area 0.2 m² separated by 0.5 mm. Calculate:
    1. The capacitance
    2. The charge stored when connected to a 12 V battery
    3. The energy stored
  7. An RC circuit has R = 2 kΩ and C = 100 μF. Calculate:
    1. The time constant
    2. The time it takes for the capacitor to charge to 63.2% of its maximum voltage
    3. The time it takes for the capacitor to charge to 95% of its maximum voltage
  8. Calculate the wavelength and frequency of electromagnetic waves for the following:
    1. Radio waves with \(\lambda = 300\) m
    2. Microwaves with \(f = 2.45\) GHz
    3. Visible light with \(\lambda = 550\) nm
    4. X-rays with \(f = 3 \times 10^{18}\) Hz
  9. Design a voltage divider circuit that provides exactly 6 V from a 12 V source using two resistors. Specify the resistor values and calculate the current drawn from the source.
  10. Analyze the following circuit and find the current through each resistor: a 24 V battery in series with a 6 Ω resistor, with a parallel combination of an 8 Ω resistor in series with the battery path and a 12 Ω resistor in one branch; a second branch contains a 4 Ω resistor in series with a 10 Ω resistor.
  11. Two point charges, \(q_1 = +5.0\,\mu\text{C}\) and \(q_2 = -3.0\,\mu\text{C}\), are \(0.40\,\text{m}\) apart. Find the electric potential at a point \(0.10\,\text{m}\) from \(q_1\) and \(0.30\,\text{m}\) from \(q_2\) along the line joining them.
  12. A \(470\,\Omega\) resistor is connected across a \(9.0\,\text{V}\) battery. Find the current, the power dissipated, and the energy consumed in \(10\,\text{min}\).
  13. A wire of length \(0.80\,\text{m}\) carries \(5.0\,\text{A}\) at \(30^\circ\) to a \(0.40\,\text{T}\) magnetic field. Find the magnetic force on the wire.
  14. A step-up transformer has \(150\) primary turns and \(1200\) secondary turns. If the primary voltage is \(120\,\text{V}\) and the secondary current is \(0.50\,\text{A}\), find the secondary voltage and the primary current.
  15. A \(220\,\mu\text{F}\) capacitor is charged to \(24\,\text{V}\). Find the stored charge and energy.
  16. An RC circuit has \(R = 5.0\,\text{k}\Omega\) and \(C = 20\,\mu\text{F}\). Find the time constant and the time for the capacitor to reach \(90\%\) of its final charge.
