Chapter 13: Cross-Cutting Application of Physics

Welcome to Cross-Cutting Applications of Physics

Physics rarely stops at the equations on the page. The same physical laws reappear in agriculture, industry, medicine, archaeology, and the study of Earth and space. Recognizing the underlying principle lets us apply tools from mechanics, electricity, waves, and thermodynamics to unfamiliar contexts.

This chapter examines how energy balance, orbital mechanics, radioactivity, medical imaging, and signal analysis show up in real-world systems, and how applied-physics reasoning turns context into solvable problems.

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Understand how physics principles apply to agricultural systems and soil science
  • Analyze the role of physics in various industrial processes and technologies
  • Explain the physics behind medical imaging techniques and radiation therapy
  • Describe how physics methods are used in archaeological research and dating
  • Apply physics concepts to earth and space sciences, including satellite orbits
  • Understand the physics principles behind various power generation methods
  • Analyze the environmental impacts of different energy technologies
  • Apply physics concepts to solve interdisciplinary problems

13.1 Application in Agriculture

Definition

Agricultural physics is the application of physics principles and methods to agricultural systems, including soil science, plant physiology, and agricultural engineering.

Application

Physics applications in agriculture include:

  • Soil moisture measurement and management
  • Irrigation system design and optimization
  • Greenhouse climate control
  • Precision agriculture using GPS and sensors
  • Post-harvest technology and storage

13.1.1 Energy Balance in Soils

Key Concept

The energy balance in soils is governed by the first law of thermodynamics:

\(Q = mc\Delta T\)

where:

  • \(Q\) is the heat energy transferred
  • \(m\) is the mass of soil
  • \(c\) is the specific heat capacity of soil
  • \(\Delta T\) is the temperature change
Important

The surface energy balance equation:

\(R_n = H + LE + G\)

where:

  • \(R_n\) is the net radiation at the surface
  • \(H\) is the sensible heat flux to the atmosphere
  • \(LE\) is the latent heat flux (evaporation)
  • \(G\) is the soil heat flux
Figure 13.1: Surface energy balance of a soil
Example

Calculate the heat required to raise the temperature of 1 hectare of soil (to 15 cm depth) by 5°C. Assume soil density of 1.3 g/cm³ and specific heat capacity of 0.8 J/g°C.

Solution

First, calculate the mass of soil:

\(\text{Area} = 1\,\text{hectare} = 10{,}000\,\text{m}^2 = 10^8\,\text{cm}^2\)
\(\text{Volume} = \text{Area} \times \text{depth} = 10^8\,\text{cm}^2 \times 15\,\text{cm} = 1.5 \times 10^9\,\text{cm}^3\)
\(\text{Mass} = \text{Density} \times \text{Volume} = 1.3\,\text{g/cm}^3 \times 1.5 \times 10^9\,\text{cm}^3 = 1.95 \times 10^9\,\text{g}\)

Now calculate the heat required:

\(Q = mc\Delta T = (1.95 \times 10^9\,\text{g}) \times (0.8\,\text{J\,g}^{-1}\,^{\circ}\text{C}^{-1}) \times (5\,^{\circ}\text{C}) = 7.8 \times 10^9\,\text{J}\)

13.1.2 Physics of Soils

Definition

Soil physics involves the study of physical properties and processes of soils, including:

  • Soil texture and structure
  • Water retention and movement
  • Heat transfer
  • Aeration and gas exchange
  • Mechanical properties
Important

Darcy's Law for water flow in soils:

\(Q = -KA\frac{dh}{dl}\)

where:

  • \(Q\) is the discharge rate (m³/s)
  • \(K\) is the hydraulic conductivity (m/s)
  • \(A\) is the cross-sectional area (m²)
  • \(\frac{dh}{dl}\) is the hydraulic gradient
Figure 13.2: Darcy's law for water flow through a soil column
Key Concept

Soil water potential components:

  • Gravitational potential: \(\psi_g = \rho g h\)
  • Matric potential: \(\psi_m\) (due to soil matrix forces)
  • Osmotic potential: \(\psi_o\) (due to dissolved salts)

Total water potential: \(\psi_t = \psi_g + \psi_m + \psi_o\)

Practice Problem

A soil sample has a bulk density of 1.2 g/cm³ and particle density of 2.65 g/cm³. Calculate the porosity of the soil.

Solution

Porosity is the fraction of the total volume occupied by pore space:

\(\phi = 1 - \frac{\rho_{\text{bulk}}}{\rho_{\text{particle}}} = 1 - \frac{1.2}{2.65} \approx 0.55\)

About \(55\%\) of the soil volume is pore space, which can hold air or water. This high porosity is typical of well-aggregated agricultural soil and is important for root respiration and water storage.

13.2 Physics and Industries

Definition

Industrial physics applies physics principles to solve problems in industrial settings and develop new technologies.

Application

Physics applications in industries include:

  • Materials science and engineering
  • Quality control and non-destructive testing
  • Process optimization and control
  • Energy efficiency and conservation
  • Automation and robotics
Important

Non-destructive testing methods:

  • Ultrasonic testing: Uses high-frequency sound waves
  • Radiographic testing: Uses X-rays or gamma rays
  • Magnetic particle testing: For ferromagnetic materials
  • Liquid penetrant testing: For surface defects
  • Eddy current testing: For conductive materials
Figure 13.3: Ultrasonic testing of a steel component
Example

In ultrasonic testing, a 5 MHz pulse is used to inspect a steel component. If the speed of sound in steel is 5960 m/s, calculate the wavelength of the ultrasound.

Solution
\(\lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{5960\,\text{m/s}}{5 \times 10^6\,\text{Hz}} = 1.192 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m} = 1.192\,\text{mm}\)
Key Concept

Process control in industries often uses feedback systems based on:

  • Temperature sensors (thermocouples, RTDs)
  • Pressure sensors (piezoelectric, strain gauge)
  • Flow sensors (ultrasonic, electromagnetic)
  • Level sensors (capacitive, ultrasonic)
  • Analytical instruments (spectroscopy, chromatography)

13.3 Physics in Health Sciences and Medical Imaging

13.3.1 Radioactivity

Definition

Radioactivity is the process by which unstable atomic nuclei emit radiation to become more stable.

Important

Types of radioactive decay:

  • Alpha decay: Emission of alpha particles (\(^4_2\)He nuclei)
  • Beta decay: Emission of electrons or positrons
  • Gamma decay: Emission of high-energy photons
Important

The radioactive decay law:

\(N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}\)

where:

  • \(N\) is the number of radioactive atoms at time \(t\)
  • \(N_0\) is the initial number of radioactive atoms
  • \(\lambda\) is the decay constant

Half-life: \(t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}\)

Figure 13.4: Exponential decay of a radioactive sample
Example

A radioactive isotope has a half-life of 8 days. What fraction of the original material remains after 24 days?

Solution

Number of half-lives: \(\frac{24}{8} = 3\)

\(\text{Fraction remaining} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 = \frac{1}{8} = 0.125 = 12.5\%\)

13.3.2 Health Effects of Radiation

Definition

Radiation dose measures the amount of radiation energy absorbed by tissue:

  • Absorbed dose: Gray (Gy) = 1 J/kg
  • Equivalent dose: Sievert (Sv) = Gy × radiation weighting factor
Important

Radiation protection principles:

  • Time: Minimize exposure time
  • Distance: Maximize distance from source
  • Shielding: Use appropriate shielding materials
Example

A patient receives an absorbed dose of \(2.0\,\text{mGy}\) from alpha particles, which have a radiation weighting factor of \(20\). Find the equivalent dose.

Solution
\(H = D\,w_R = (2.0\,\text{mGy})(20) = 40\,\text{mSv}\)

Although the absorbed energy is small, alpha particles deposit it densely along their short tracks, so the biological effect is much larger than for the same absorbed dose of X-rays.

Key Concept

Biological effects of radiation:

  • Deterministic effects: Occur above threshold doses (e.g., radiation sickness)
  • Stochastic effects: Probability increases with dose (e.g., cancer)

13.3.3 Medical Imaging

Definition

Medical imaging uses various physical principles to create visual representations of the interior of the body.

Important

Common medical imaging techniques:

  • X-ray imaging: Uses electromagnetic radiation
  • Computed Tomography (CT): Combines multiple X-ray measurements
  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): Uses nuclear magnetic resonance
  • Ultrasound: Uses high-frequency sound waves
  • Nuclear medicine: Uses radioactive tracers
Figure 13.5: Basic principle of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)
Example

In MRI, the Larmor frequency is given by \(\omega = \gamma B\), where \(\gamma\) is the gyromagnetic ratio. For hydrogen nuclei, \(\gamma = 42.58\) MHz/T. Calculate the resonance frequency in a 1.5 T MRI scanner.

Solution
\(f = \gamma B = (42.58\,\text{MHz/T}) \times (1.5\,\text{T}) = 63.87\,\text{MHz}\)
Application

Physics in therapy:

  • Radiation therapy: Uses ionizing radiation to treat cancer
  • Hyperthermia: Uses heat to treat tumors
  • Photodynamic therapy: Uses light-activated drugs
  • Ultrasound therapy: Uses focused ultrasound for various treatments

13.4 Physics and Archaeology

Definition

Archaeological physics applies physics methods to study archaeological materials and sites.

Application

Physics techniques in archaeology:

  • Radiocarbon dating: Determines the age of organic materials
  • Thermoluminescence dating: Dates pottery and burnt stones
  • Dendrochronology: Uses tree-ring patterns for dating
  • Ground-penetrating radar: Maps subsurface features
  • X-ray fluorescence: Analyzes elemental composition
Important

Radiocarbon dating principle:

  • Living organisms maintain a constant \(^{14}\text{C}/^{12}\text{C}\) ratio
  • After death, \(^{14}\text{C}\) decays with half-life of 5730 years
  • Measuring the remaining \(^{14}\text{C}\) gives the age
Figure 13.6: Radiocarbon decay and archaeological dating
Example

An archaeological sample has \(^{14}\text{C}\) activity that is 25% of modern standards. Estimate the age of the sample.

Solution

Fraction remaining = 0.25

Number of half-lives: \(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n = 0.25 \Rightarrow n = 2\)

\(\text{Age} = n \times t_{1/2} = 2 \times 5730 = 11{,}460\,\text{years}\)
Key Concept

Other dating methods:

  • Potassium-argon dating: For volcanic rocks (half-life: 1.3 billion years)
  • Uranium-series dating: For carbonate materials
  • Electron spin resonance: For tooth enamel and shells
  • Archaeomagnetic dating: Based on Earth's magnetic field variations

13.5 Application in Earth and Space Sciences

Definition

Geophysics applies physics principles to study the Earth, while space physics studies physical phenomena in space.

Application

Physics applications in earth and space sciences:

  • Seismology: Study of earthquakes and seismic waves
  • Geodesy: Measurement and representation of Earth
  • Atmospheric physics: Study of atmospheric phenomena
  • Planetary science: Study of planets and solar systems
  • Astrophysics: Study of celestial objects and phenomena
Important

Seismic wave types:

  • P-waves: Primary, compressional waves (fastest)
  • S-waves: Secondary, shear waves (slower)
  • Surface waves: Travel along Earth's surface (most destructive)

13.5.1 Satellite Orbits

Definition

A satellite orbit is the curved path of a satellite around a planet or other celestial body.

Important

Types of satellite orbits:

  • Low Earth Orbit (LEO): 160–2,000 km altitude
  • Medium Earth Orbit (MEO): 2,000–35,786 km altitude
  • Geostationary Orbit (GEO): 35,786 km altitude, stationary relative to Earth's surface
  • Polar Orbit: Passes over or near both poles
Key Concept

Orbital velocity for a circular orbit:

\(v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}\)

where:

  • \(G\) is the gravitational constant
  • \(M\) is the mass of the central body
  • \(r\) is the orbital radius
Figure 13.7: Satellite in a circular orbit around Earth
Example

Calculate the orbital velocity of a satellite in circular orbit 400 km above Earth's surface. Earth's radius = 6371 km, mass = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg.

Solution

Orbital radius: \(r = 6371 + 400 = 6771\) km = \(6.771 \times 10^6\) m

\(v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11}) \times (5.97 \times 10^{24})}{6.771 \times 10^6}}\)
\(= \sqrt{\frac{3.982 \times 10^{14}}{6.771 \times 10^6}} = \sqrt{5.882 \times 10^7} = 7670\,\text{m/s}\)

13.5.2 Application in Power Generation

Definition

Power generation involves converting various forms of energy into electrical energy using physical principles.

Important

Major power generation methods:

  • Thermal power: Converts heat to electricity (Rankine cycle)
  • Hydroelectric: Uses gravitational potential energy of water
  • Nuclear: Uses nuclear fission or fusion
  • Solar: Converts sunlight to electricity (photovoltaic or thermal)
  • Wind: Converts kinetic energy of wind to electricity
Key Concept

Energy conversion efficiency:

\(\eta = \frac{\text{Useful energy output}}{\text{Total energy input}} \times 100\%\)

For a heat engine operating between a hot reservoir at absolute temperature \(T_h\) and a cold reservoir at \(T_c\), the Carnot efficiency sets the upper limit:

\(\eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{T_c}{T_h}\)

No real engine can exceed this value; irreversibilities such as friction, heat leakage, and finite-rate heat transfer always reduce the efficiency below it.

Example

A geothermal plant uses hot water at \(180^\circ\text{C}\) and discharges it at \(80^\circ\text{C}\). Find the maximum possible (Carnot) efficiency.

Solution

Convert the temperatures to kelvin:

\(T_h = 180 + 273 = 453\,\text{K}, \qquad T_c = 80 + 273 = 353\,\text{K}\)
\(\eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{353}{453} \approx 0.22 = 22\%\)

At best, about \(22\%\) of the extracted heat could become electrical work; a real plant will do somewhat worse because of pump losses, pipe friction, and imperfect insulation.

Figure 13.8: Wind turbine converting kinetic energy into electrical power
Example

A wind turbine with rotor diameter 80 m operates in wind with speed 12 m/s and density 1.2 kg/m³. If the power coefficient is 0.4, calculate the electrical power output.

Solution

First, calculate the swept area:

\(A = \pi r^2 = \pi (40)^2 = 5026.55\,\text{m}^2\)

Wind power available:

\(P_{\text{wind}} = \frac{1}{2} \rho A v^3 = \frac{1}{2} \times 1.2 \times 5026.55 \times (12)^3\)
\(= 0.6 \times 5026.55 \times 1728 = 0.6 \times 8{,}685{,}878.4 = 5{,}211{,}527\,\text{W}\)

Electrical power output:

\(P_{\text{elec}} = C_p \times P_{\text{wind}} = 0.4 \times 5{,}211{,}527 = 2{,}084{,}611\,\text{W} \approx 2.08\,\text{MW}\)
Important

Environmental considerations in power generation:

  • Carbon emissions and climate change
  • Air and water pollution
  • Land use and habitat impact
  • Waste management (especially for nuclear)
  • Resource depletion
Example

A satellite of mass 500 kg is in a circular low-Earth orbit at a radius of \(6.4 \times 10^6\,\text{m}\) from Earth's center. Find its orbital speed and its orbital period.

Solution

The orbital speed follows from gravity providing the centripetal force:

\(v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24})}{6.4 \times 10^6}} \approx 7.9 \times 10^3\,\text{m/s}\)
\(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{(6.4 \times 10^6)^3}{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24})}} \approx 5.1 \times 10^3\,\text{s} \approx 85\,\text{min}\)

Problem-Solving Workshop

Applied-physics problems often look unfamiliar because they are dressed in the language of farming, medicine, archaeology, or energy. The first step is to strip away the context and identify the underlying principle: radioactive decay, wave propagation, energy conversion, or orbital motion. Then list the given data, convert everything to SI units, and decide what quantity the application actually needs. A sketch, a table of values, or a simple block diagram can prevent you from losing track of the physical situation.

Many of these problems are multi-step. A decay problem may require a fraction, a number of half-lives, and then an age; an energy problem may require input power, efficiency, and operating time. Keep the units attached at every stage, use powers of ten carefully, and finish by translating the number back into the original context. An answer such as \(1.44\,\text{kWh}\) becomes meaningful when you explain what appliance it could run or what fraction of a household demand it supplies.

Problem-Solving Checklist
  1. Identify the physical principle behind the application before choosing an equation.
  2. List the given values and the required unknown, converting all data to SI units.
  3. Choose the appropriate model, such as exponential decay, wave travel, or energy conversion.
  4. Carry units and powers of ten through every step of the calculation.
  5. Check the result against limiting cases and common sense for the application.
  6. State the final answer with units and interpret what it means in the real-world setting.
Worked Example

A rooftop solar panel has an area of \(1.6\,\text{m}^2\) and an efficiency of \(18\%\). The solar irradiance is \(1000\,\text{W/m}^2\), and the panel receives this irradiance for \(5.0\,\text{h}\) on a clear day. Find the electrical power output and the daily electrical energy in kWh. If a household uses \(12\,\text{kWh}\) per day, what fraction of that demand does the panel supply?

Solution

The solar power incident on the panel is the irradiance multiplied by the panel area:

\(P_{\text{in}} = IA = (1000\,\text{W/m}^2)(1.6\,\text{m}^2) = 1600\,\text{W} = 1.6\,\text{kW}\)

The panel converts \(18\%\) of this incident power into electrical power:

\(P_{\text{elec}} = \eta P_{\text{in}} = (0.18)(1600\,\text{W}) = 288\,\text{W} \approx 0.288\,\text{kW}\)

Over \(5.0\,\text{h}\), the electrical energy delivered is:

\(E = P_{\text{elec}}t = (0.288\,\text{kW})(5.0\,\text{h}) = 1.44\,\text{kWh}\)

The fraction of the household's daily demand supplied by this panel is:

\(\text{Fraction} = \frac{1.44\,\text{kWh}}{12\,\text{kWh}} = 0.12 = 12\%\)

The panel delivers about \(288\,\text{W}\) and \(1.44\,\text{kWh}\) per day, which is roughly \(12\%\) of the household's \(12\,\text{kWh}\) daily use under these ideal conditions.

Why This Matters

Solar installers use exactly this calculation to size rooftop systems. A single \(1.6\,\text{m}^2\) panel supplying \(1.44\,\text{kWh}\) per day would need to be combined with several others to cover a typical household demand. Real output is lower than the ideal estimate because of cloudy weather, shallow sunlight angles, dust, temperature effects, and inverter losses, so designers add a safety margin when planning an array.

Key Takeaways

  • Applied physics connects fundamental principles to agriculture, industry, medicine, archaeology, Earth science, and energy technology.
  • Soil energy balance is described by \(Q=mc\Delta T\) and by the surface balance \(R_n=H+LE+G\).
  • Radioactive decay follows \(N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}\), with half-life \(t_{1/2}=\ln 2/\lambda\) and a remaining fraction \((1/2)^n\) after \(n\) half-lives.
  • Radiation dose is measured in gray for absorbed energy and sievert for biological effect, and protection relies on time, distance, and shielding.
  • Medical imaging uses distinct physical processes: X-ray attenuation, CT reconstruction, MRI nuclear resonance, and ultrasound echoes.
  • Radiocarbon dating uses the \(5730\)-year half-life of carbon-14, while other methods suit rocks, pottery, and tooth enamel.
  • Satellites obey \(v=\sqrt{GM/r}\) for circular orbits, and power systems are compared by their conversion efficiency \(\eta=E_{\text{out}}/E_{\text{in}}\).
