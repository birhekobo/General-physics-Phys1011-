Chapter 10: Oscillations and Waves

Welcome to Oscillations and Waves

Oscillations and waves are the natural way that systems return to equilibrium and how disturbances propagate through fields and media. Simple harmonic motion describes any system where the restoring force is proportional to displacement, producing sinusoidal motion with a characteristic period.

Energy in an oscillator is continuously exchanged between kinetic and potential forms while the total mechanical energy stays constant. Extending this idea to waves, we find that waves transport energy through a medium without transporting matter itself, giving rise to standing waves, resonance, and the Doppler effect.

10.1 Simple Harmonic Motion

Definition

Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) is a type of periodic motion where the restoring force is directly proportional to the displacement and acts in the direction opposite to that of displacement.

The general equation for SHM is:

\(x(t) = A \cos(\omega t + \phi)\)

where:

  • \(x(t)\) is the displacement at time \(t\)
  • \(A\) is the amplitude (maximum displacement)
  • \(\omega\) is the angular frequency
  • \(\phi\) is the phase constant

10.1.1 Motion of an Object Attached to a Spring

Key Concept

For a mass-spring system, the restoring force is given by Hooke's Law:

\(F = -kx\)

where \(k\) is the spring constant and \(x\) is the displacement from equilibrium.

Using Newton's second law (\(F = ma\)), we derive:

\(m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -kx\)
\(\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + \frac{k}{m}x = 0\)

This is the differential equation for SHM, with solution:

\(x(t) = A \cos(\omega t + \phi)\)

where the angular frequency is:

\(\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\)

The period and frequency are:

\(T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}, \quad f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\)
Figure 10.1: Mass-spring system executing simple harmonic motion
Example

A 0.5 kg mass is attached to a spring with spring constant 200 N/m. Find the period and frequency of oscillation.

Solution
\(\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{200}{0.5}} = \sqrt{400} = 20\,\text{rad/s}\)
\(T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \frac{2\pi}{20} = \frac{\pi}{10} \approx 0.314\,\text{s}\)
\(f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{10}{\pi} \approx 3.18\,\text{Hz}\)

10.1.2 Energy of Simple Harmonic Oscillator

Key Concept

In SHM, energy continuously transforms between kinetic and potential forms, with the total mechanical energy remaining constant.

The potential energy stored in a spring is:

\(U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 \cos^2(\omega t + \phi)\)

The kinetic energy of the oscillating mass is:

\(K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 A^2 \sin^2(\omega t + \phi)\)

Since \(\omega^2 = k/m\), we can write:

\(K = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 \sin^2(\omega t + \phi)\)

The total mechanical energy is:

\(E = U + K = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 \cos^2(\omega t + \phi) + \frac{1}{2}kA^2 \sin^2(\omega t + \phi) = \frac{1}{2}kA^2\)
Important

The total energy in SHM is constant and proportional to the square of the amplitude.

Example

A 0.2 kg mass oscillates with amplitude 0.1 m on a spring with \(k = 50\) N/m. Find:

a) The total energy
b) The maximum speed
c) The speed when the displacement is 0.05 m

Solution

a) Total energy:

\(E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 = \frac{1}{2}(50)(0.1)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(50)(0.01) = 0.25\,\text{J}\)

b) Maximum speed occurs when all energy is kinetic:

\(\frac{1}{2}mv_{\text{max}}^2 = E \;\Rightarrow\; v_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{\frac{2E}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(0.25)}{0.2}} = \sqrt{2.5} \approx 1.58\,\text{m/s}\)

c) When \(x = 0.05\) m:

\(U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}(50)(0.05)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(50)(0.0025) = 0.0625\,\text{J}\)
\(K = E - U = 0.25 - 0.0625 = 0.1875\,\text{J}\)
\(v = \sqrt{\frac{2K}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(0.1875)}{0.2}} = \sqrt{1.875} \approx 1.37\,\text{m/s}\)
Example

A 0.5 kg mass oscillates on a spring with \(k = 200\,\text{N/m}\) and amplitude \(A = 0.15\,\text{m}\). Find the total mechanical energy and the maximum speed of the oscillation.

Solution

The total mechanical energy of the oscillator is

\(E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 = \frac{1}{2}(200)(0.15)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(200)(0.0225) = 2.25\,\text{J}\)

The maximum speed occurs when all energy is kinetic:

\(K = E = \frac{1}{2}mv_{\text{max}}^2 \;\Rightarrow\; v_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{\frac{2E}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(2.25)}{0.5}} = \sqrt{9} = 3\,\text{m/s}\)

10.1.3 Comparing Simple Harmonic Motion with Uniform Circular Motion

Key Concept

There is a fundamental relationship between simple harmonic motion and uniform circular motion. The projection of uniform circular motion onto any diameter executes simple harmonic motion.

Consider a particle moving with constant angular velocity \(\omega\) in a circle of radius \(A\). Its x-coordinate is:

\(x = A \cos(\omega t + \phi)\)

which is identical to the equation for SHM.

Figure 10.2: Reference circle connecting uniform circular motion and SHM
Important

The reference circle method provides a geometric interpretation of SHM:

  • The amplitude \(A\) corresponds to the radius of the circle
  • The phase angle \((\omega t + \phi)\) corresponds to the angular position
  • The angular frequency \(\omega\) corresponds to the angular velocity

10.1.4 The Simple Pendulum

Definition

A simple pendulum consists of a point mass suspended from a fixed support by a massless, inextensible string.

For small angles (\(\theta < 10^\circ\)), the motion is approximately SHM with:

\(\omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}, \quad T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}, \quad f = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}\)

where \(L\) is the length of the pendulum and \(g\) is the acceleration due to gravity.

Figure 10.3: Simple pendulum and forces acting on the bob
Example

Calculate the period of a simple pendulum with length 1.0 m.

Solution
\(T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{1.0}{9.8}} \approx 2\pi \sqrt{0.102} \approx 2\pi(0.319) \approx 2.00\,\text{s}\)
Important

The period of a simple pendulum depends only on its length and the gravitational acceleration, not on the mass of the bob.

10.1.5 Damped and Driven Oscillations

Real oscillators lose mechanical energy to friction and air resistance, so their amplitude gradually decreases. This is called damping. For light damping, the motion still oscillates but with an amplitude that decays exponentially:

\(x(t) = A_0 e^{-bt/(2m)} \cos(\omega' t + \phi)\)

where \(b\) is the damping constant. With heavy damping, the system may return to equilibrium without oscillating at all (overdamping), or just barely without crossing equilibrium (critical damping). Car shock absorbers are designed near critical damping so the vehicle settles quickly without bouncing.

If a periodic driving force is applied, the system eventually oscillates at the driving frequency rather than its natural frequency. When the driving frequency matches the natural frequency, the amplitude becomes very large: this is resonance. The sharpness of the resonance depends on the amount of damping. A swing pushed at just the right moments is a familiar example, while the collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in 1940 illustrates how resonant vibration can destroy a structure.

10.2 Resonance

Definition

Resonance occurs when a system is driven at its natural frequency, resulting in a large amplitude response.

Application

Resonance phenomena are observed in many systems:

  • Musical instruments (strings, air columns)
  • Buildings and bridges during earthquakes
  • Radio and TV receivers (tuning circuits)
  • MRI machines in medical imaging
Figure 10.4: Resonance curves for different amounts of damping
Important

Resonance can be beneficial (musical instruments) or destructive (bridge collapse). Engineers must consider resonance effects when designing structures.

10.3 Mechanical Waves

Definition

A mechanical wave is a disturbance that travels through a medium, transporting energy from one point to another without transporting matter.

10.3.1 Wave Types and Propagation of a Disturbance

Key Concept

There are two main types of mechanical waves:

  • Transverse waves: The particle motion is perpendicular to the wave direction (e.g., waves on a string)
  • Longitudinal waves: The particle motion is parallel to the wave direction (e.g., sound waves in air)

Wave characteristics:

  • Wavelength (\(\lambda\)): Distance between successive identical points on the wave
  • Frequency (\(f\)): Number of oscillations per unit time
  • Period (\(T\)): Time for one complete oscillation (\(T = 1/f\))
  • Amplitude (\(A\)): Maximum displacement from equilibrium
  • Wave speed (\(v\)): Speed at which the wave propagates (\(v = \lambda f\))
Figure 10.5: Transverse and longitudinal waves with their characteristics

10.3.2 A Travelling Wave

The mathematical description of a sinusoidal wave traveling in the positive x-direction is:

\(y(x,t) = A \sin(kx - \omega t + \phi)\)

where:

  • \(y(x,t)\) is the displacement at position \(x\) and time \(t\)
  • \(A\) is the amplitude
  • \(k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\) is the wave number
  • \(\omega = 2\pi f\) is the angular frequency
  • \(\phi\) is the phase constant

The wave speed is given by:

\(v = \lambda f = \frac{\omega}{k}\)
Example

A wave is described by \(y(x,t) = 0.2 \sin(2x - 4t)\), where \(x\) and \(y\) are in meters and \(t\) is in seconds. Find:

a) The amplitude
b) The wavelength
c) The period
d) The wave speed

Solution

Comparing with the general form \(y(x,t) = A \sin(kx - \omega t)\):

a) Amplitude \(A = 0.2\) m

b) Wave number \(k = 2\) rad/m, so \(\lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k} = \frac{2\pi}{2} = \pi\) m

c) Angular frequency \(\omega = 4\) rad/s, so \(T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \frac{2\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{2}\) s

d) Wave speed \(v = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{4}{2} = 2\) m/s

10.3.3 Energy and Intensity of Waves

A wave carries energy through a medium. For a mechanical wave, the energy transported per unit time is proportional to the square of the amplitude and to the square of the frequency. The intensity \(I\) is the power transmitted per unit area perpendicular to the direction of propagation:

\(I = \frac{P}{A}\)

For a point source radiating uniformly in three dimensions, the energy spreads over a spherical surface of area \(4\pi r^2\), so the intensity decreases with distance as:

\(I = \frac{P}{4\pi r^2}\)

This is the inverse-square law: doubling the distance reduces the intensity to one quarter.

Example

A small loudspeaker emits sound uniformly in all directions with a power of \(2.0\,\text{W}\). Find the sound intensity at a distance of \(4.0\,\text{m}\).

Solution

Using the inverse-square law:

\(I = \frac{P}{4\pi r^2} = \frac{2.0}{4\pi(4.0)^2} = \frac{2.0}{201} \approx 1.0\times10^{-2}\,\text{W/m}^2\)

At twice the distance, \(8.0\,\text{m}\), the intensity would fall to \(2.5\times10^{-3}\,\text{W/m}^2\).

10.4 Standing Waves

Definition

Standing waves are wave patterns that result from the interference of two waves of identical frequency traveling in opposite directions. They appear to be stationary.

The equation for a standing wave is:

\(y(x,t) = 2A \sin(kx) \cos(\omega t)\)
Important

Standing waves have nodes (points of zero amplitude) and antinodes (points of maximum amplitude) at fixed positions.

Figure 10.6: Standing wave on a string fixed at both ends

10.4.1 Standing Waves in a String

For a string fixed at both ends, standing waves occur at specific frequencies called harmonics:

\(f_n = \frac{n}{2L} \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\)

where:

  • \(L\) is the length of the string
  • \(T\) is the tension in the string
  • \(\mu\) is the linear mass density

The wavelength of the \(n\)th harmonic is:

\(\lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}\)
Example

A guitar string of length 0.65 m has a linear mass density of \(3.5 \times 10^{-3}\) kg/m. If the tension is 150 N, find the fundamental frequency.

Solution
\(f_1 = \frac{1}{2L} \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} = \frac{1}{2(0.65)} \sqrt{\frac{150}{3.5 \times 10^{-3}}} = \frac{1}{1.3} \sqrt{42857.14}\)
\(= 0.769 \times 207.0 \approx 159.2\,\text{Hz}\)

10.4.2 Standing Waves in Air Columns

Standing waves can form in air columns in pipes:

  • Open at both ends: All harmonics are present (\(f_n = \frac{nv}{2L}\), \(n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\))
  • Closed at one end: Only odd harmonics are present (\(f_n = \frac{nv}{4L}\), \(n = 1, 3, 5, \ldots\))
Figure 10.7: Standing wave patterns in open and closed air columns
Example

A pipe open at both ends is 1.0 m long. The speed of sound is 340 m/s. Find the fundamental frequency and the first two overtones.

Solution

Fundamental frequency:

\(f_1 = \frac{v}{2L} = \frac{340}{2(1.0)} = 170\,\text{Hz}\)

First overtone (second harmonic):

\(f_2 = 2f_1 = 340\,\text{Hz}\)

Second overtone (third harmonic):

\(f_3 = 3f_1 = 510\,\text{Hz}\)

10.5 The Doppler Effect

Definition

The Doppler effect is the change in frequency or wavelength of a wave in relation to an observer who is moving relative to the wave source.

For sound waves, when the source and observer are moving along the line connecting them:

\(f' = f \left( \frac{v \pm v_o}{v \mp v_s} \right)\)

where:

  • \(f'\) is the observed frequency
  • \(f\) is the source frequency
  • \(v\) is the speed of sound
  • \(v_o\) is the speed of the observer
  • \(v_s\) is the speed of the source

Use the upper signs (+) in numerator and (−) in denominator if the observer is moving toward the source or the source is moving toward the observer.

Figure 10.8: Doppler effect for an ambulance approaching a stationary observer
Example

An ambulance with siren emitting at 1000 Hz moves toward a stationary observer at 30 m/s. The speed of sound is 340 m/s. What frequency does the observer hear?

Solution

The source is moving toward the observer, so:

\(f' = f \left( \frac{v}{v - v_s} \right) = 1000 \left( \frac{340}{340 - 30} \right) = 1000 \left( \frac{340}{310} \right) \approx 1097\,\text{Hz}\)
Example

An observer moves toward a stationary source emitting sound at \(500\,\text{Hz}\) with a speed of \(25\,\text{m/s}\). The speed of sound is \(340\,\text{m/s}\). Find the observed frequency.

Solution

The observer is moving toward the source, so the numerator uses the plus sign:

\(f' = f\left(\frac{v + v_o}{v}\right) = 500\left(\frac{340 + 25}{340}\right) = 500\left(\frac{365}{340}\right) \approx 537\,\text{Hz}\)

The observer encounters more wave crests per second, so the pitch is higher. If the observer moved away at the same speed, the frequency would fall to about \(463\,\text{Hz}\).

Important

For electromagnetic waves (light, radio waves), the Doppler effect formula is different due to relativistic effects:

\(f' = f \sqrt{\frac{1 + v/c}{1 - v/c}}\)

for relative approach, where \(c\) is the speed of light.

Example

A galaxy emits light at a frequency of \(5.0 \times 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}\). If the galaxy is moving away from Earth at \(0.2c\), what frequency does an Earth-based observer measure? (Use the relativistic Doppler formula.)

Solution

Because the source is receding, use the redshift form of the relativistic Doppler formula:

\(f' = f\sqrt{\frac{1 - v/c}{1 + v/c}} = (5.0 \times 10^{14})\sqrt{\frac{1 - 0.2}{1 + 0.2}} = (5.0 \times 10^{14})\sqrt{\frac{0.8}{1.2}} \approx 4.08 \times 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}\)

The observed frequency is lower (redshifted) because the source is moving away from the observer.

Problem-Solving Workshop

Oscillation and wave problems become manageable once you identify the physical system and the quantity the question asks for. Begin by deciding whether you are treating a mass on a spring, a pendulum, a wave on a string, or an air column. Then list the known values in SI units, sketch the equilibrium position and the displacement, and mark the direction of the restoring force. For oscillations, the key parameters are mass, spring constant or pendulum length, amplitude, period, and frequency; for waves they are speed, wavelength, frequency, and the boundary conditions at each end.

Standing-wave problems are really boundary-condition problems. A fixed end must be a node, an open end must be an antinode, and the allowed wavelengths follow from fitting the pattern into the available length. Once the wavelength is known, \(v=\lambda f\) converts between wavelength and frequency. Always check that overtones lie above the fundamental, that only permitted harmonics appear, and that the final unit is hertz or metres as requested.

Problem-Solving Checklist
  1. Identify the oscillating or wave system and list the known quantities in SI units.
  2. For SHM, find \(\omega\), \(T\), and \(f\) from \(k/m\) or \(L/g\), and confirm that the small-angle approximation is valid for a pendulum.
  3. For energy questions, use \(E=\frac{1}{2}kA^2\) and conserve \(K+U\) at two positions.
  4. For travelling waves, relate speed, wavelength, and frequency with \(v=\lambda f\).
  5. For standing waves, identify nodes and antinodes first, then select the allowed harmonics.
  6. Check units, significant figures, and whether each overtone is higher than the fundamental.
Worked Example

A pipe closed at one end and open at the other is \(0.85\,\text{m}\) long. The speed of sound is \(340\,\text{m/s}\). Find the fundamental frequency and wavelength, and the frequency and wavelength of the first two overtones.

Solution

A pipe closed at one end supports only odd harmonics. The fundamental wavelength is four times the pipe length:

\(\lambda_1 = 4L = 4(0.85\,\text{m}) = 3.40\,\text{m}\)

The fundamental frequency is therefore:

\(f_1 = \frac{v}{\lambda_1} = \frac{340\,\text{m/s}}{3.40\,\text{m}} = 100\,\text{Hz}\)

The first overtone is the third harmonic, with \(n=3\):

\(f_3 = 3f_1 = 3(100\,\text{Hz}) = 300\,\text{Hz}, \qquad \lambda_3 = \frac{4L}{3} = \frac{3.40\,\text{m}}{3} \approx 1.13\,\text{m}\)

The second overtone is the fifth harmonic, with \(n=5\):

\(f_5 = 5f_1 = 5(100\,\text{Hz}) = 500\,\text{Hz}, \qquad \lambda_5 = \frac{4L}{5} = \frac{3.40\,\text{m}}{5} = 0.680\,\text{m}\)

The even harmonics are absent, and the predicted frequencies are \(100\,\text{Hz}\), \(300\,\text{Hz}\), and \(500\,\text{Hz}\), with wavelengths \(3.40\,\text{m}\), \(1.13\,\text{m}\), and \(0.680\,\text{m}\).

Why This Matters

A clarinet behaves approximately like a pipe closed at one end, which is why its lowest register emphasizes odd harmonics and has a distinctive hollow tone. Instrument makers use these boundary conditions when choosing bore length and tone-hole positions, while tuners rely on the same harmonic relationships to set a reliable reference pitch.

Key Takeaways

  • Simple harmonic motion occurs when the restoring force is proportional and opposite to the displacement, \(F=-kx\).
  • For a mass-spring system, \(\omega=\sqrt{k/m}\) and \(T=2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\); for a small-angle pendulum, \(T=2\pi\sqrt{L/g}\).
  • The total energy of an ideal oscillator is constant, \(E=\frac{1}{2}kA^2\), while kinetic and potential energy exchange continuously.
  • A travelling wave transports energy without transporting matter, with speed \(v=\lambda f\).
  • Standing waves have nodes and antinodes fixed by the boundaries of the medium.
  • Strings and pipes open at both ends support all harmonics, \(f_n=nv/(2L)\), while a pipe closed at one end supports only odd harmonics, \(f_n=nv/(4L)\) with \(n\) odd.
  • The Doppler effect raises the observed frequency when source and observer approach and lowers it when they separate.
