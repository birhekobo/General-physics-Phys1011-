Chapter 6: Work and Energy

Welcome to Work and Energy

Work and energy are fundamental concepts in physics that provide powerful tools for analyzing mechanical systems. While forces describe interactions, work and energy describe the effects of these interactions over time and distance.

Work is the transfer of energy through a force acting through a displacement: \(W = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{d} = Fd\cos\theta\). The work done by a conservative force equals the negative change in potential energy, and the work-energy theorem links force to motion through the kinetic energy \(K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2\).

Because mechanical energy is conserved when only conservative forces act, energy analysis often replaces force-by-force analysis. This chapter unifies work, kinetic energy, potential energy, conservation, and power into a single energy perspective.

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Understand the concept of work and calculate work done by constant forces
  • Apply the work-energy theorem to solve problems
  • Calculate kinetic energy and potential energy (elastic and gravitational)
  • Understand and apply the principle of conservation of mechanical energy
  • Solve problems involving conservative and non-conservative forces
  • Calculate power and understand its relationship to work and energy
  • Apply energy concepts to solve complex physics problems
  • Analyze systems using energy conservation principles

6.1 The Concept of Work

Work and energy are fundamental concepts in physics that provide powerful tools for analyzing mechanical systems. While forces describe interactions, work and energy describe the effects of these interactions over time and distance.

6.1.1 Work Done by a Constant Force

Definition

Work is done when a force causes displacement. For a constant force \(\vec{F}\) acting on an object that undergoes a displacement \(\vec{d}\), the work done is:

\(W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = Fd\cos\theta\)

where:

  • \(W\) = work done (joules, J)
  • \(F\) = magnitude of the force (newtons, N)
  • \(d\) = magnitude of the displacement (meters, m)
  • \(\theta\) = angle between the force and displacement vectors

1 joule = 1 newton·meter (1 J = 1 N·m)

Key Concept

Key points about work:

  • Work is a scalar quantity (has magnitude but no direction)
  • Work can be positive, negative, or zero
  • Only the component of force parallel to the displacement does work
  • No work is done if there is no displacement
  • Work is energy transferred to or from an object
Figure 6.1: Work done by a constant force
Example

A person pulls a crate with a force of 50 N at an angle of 30° above the horizontal. If the crate moves 10 m horizontally, how much work is done?

Solution
\(W = Fd\cos\theta = (50\,\text{N})(10\,\text{m})\cos 30^\circ = 500 \times 0.866 = 433\,\text{J}\)
Practice Problem

A force of 20 N is applied to push a box across a floor. The force is directed 45° below the horizontal. If the box moves 5 m, how much work is done?

Solution
\(W = Fd\cos\theta = (20\,\text{N})(5\,\text{m})\cos 45^\circ = 100 \times 0.707 \approx 71\,\text{J}\)

6.1.2 Work Done by a Variable Force

When the force is not constant, the work is found by dividing the motion into small displacements and adding the contributions. In the limit of infinitesimal steps,

\(W = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} F_x\,dx\)

Graphically, this is the area under the force-versus-position curve. A constant force gives a rectangle of area \(F\Delta x\); a force that grows linearly with displacement gives a triangle.

For an ideal spring, \(F_x = kx\), so the work done in stretching the spring from \(x_i\) to \(x_f\) is

\(W = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} kx\,dx = \frac{1}{2}kx_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}kx_i^2\)

This is precisely the change in elastic potential energy, which is why the spring force is conservative.

Example

A force acting in the \(x\)-direction increases linearly from zero to \(50\,\text{N}\) as an object moves from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 4.0\,\text{m}\). Find the work done.

Solution

The force-displacement graph is a triangle with base \(4.0\,\text{m}\) and height \(50\,\text{N}\). Its area is

\(W = \tfrac{1}{2}(\text{base})(\text{height}) = \tfrac{1}{2}(4.0)(50) = 100\,\text{J}\)

Equivalently, if \(F = (12.5\,\text{N/m})x\), then \(W = \int_0^4 12.5x\,dx = 6.25x^2\big|_0^4 = 100\,\text{J}\).

6.1.3 Kinetic Energy and Work-Energy Theorem

Definition

Kinetic energy is the energy of motion. For an object of mass \(m\) moving with speed \(v\):

\(K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\)

where:

  • \(K\) = kinetic energy (joules, J)
  • \(m\) = mass (kilograms, kg)
  • \(v\) = speed (meters per second, m/s)
Work-Energy Theorem

Work-Energy Theorem: The net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy:

\(W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2\)

This theorem provides a powerful connection between forces acting on an object and its resulting motion.

Key Concept

Key points about kinetic energy and the work-energy theorem:

  • Kinetic energy is always positive or zero
  • The work-energy theorem applies to both constant and variable forces
  • Only the net work (work done by all forces) affects the kinetic energy
  • The theorem is especially useful when acceleration is not constant
Example

A 2 kg object initially at rest is acted upon by a net force of 10 N over a distance of 5 m. What is its final speed?

Solution

Using the work-energy theorem:

\(W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2\)
\(F_{\text{net}}d = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - 0 \quad (\text{since } v_i = 0)\)
\((10\,\text{N})(5\,\text{m}) = \frac{1}{2}(2\,\text{kg})v_f^2\)
\(50 = v_f^2 \;\Rightarrow\; v_f = \sqrt{50} \approx 7.07\,\text{m/s}\)
Practice Problem

A 1000 kg car accelerates from 20 m/s to 30 m/s. How much net work is required?

Solution
\(W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1000)(30^2 - 20^2) = 500(900 - 400) = 500 \times 500 = 250{,}000\,\text{J}\)

6.1.4 Elastic Potential Energy

Definition

Elastic potential energy is the energy stored in elastic materials when they are stretched or compressed. For an ideal spring that obeys Hooke's Law:

\(U_s = \frac{1}{2}kx^2\)

where:

  • \(U_s\) = elastic potential energy (joules, J)
  • \(k\) = spring constant (newtons per meter, N/m)
  • \(x\) = displacement from equilibrium position (meters, m)

Hooke's Law: \(F = -kx\) (the restoring force is proportional to displacement)

Key Concept

Key points about elastic potential energy:

  • Elastic potential energy is always positive or zero
  • The spring constant \(k\) measures the stiffness of the spring
  • The work done in stretching or compressing a spring equals the change in elastic potential energy
  • Elastic potential energy is a form of mechanical energy
Figure 6.2: Elastic potential energy in a spring
Example

A spring with a spring constant of 200 N/m is compressed by 0.1 m. What is the elastic potential energy stored in the spring?

Solution
\(U_s = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}(200\,\text{N/m})(0.1\,\text{m})^2 = 100 \times 0.01 = 1\,\text{J}\)

6.1.5 Gravitational Potential Energy

Definition

Gravitational potential energy is the energy stored in an object due to its position in a gravitational field. Near Earth's surface:

\(U_g = mgh\)

where:

  • \(U_g\) = gravitational potential energy (joules, J)
  • \(m\) = mass (kilograms, kg)
  • \(g\) = acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s²)
  • \(h\) = height above reference level (meters, m)

The more general form for any two masses is:

\(U_g = -G\frac{m_1m_2}{r}\)
Key Concept

Key points about gravitational potential energy:

  • Gravitational potential energy depends on the reference level (where \(h = 0\))
  • Only changes in potential energy are physically significant
  • The work done against gravity equals the change in gravitational potential energy
  • Gravitational potential energy is a form of mechanical energy
Figure 6.3: Gravitational potential energy at different heights
Example

A 5 kg object is lifted from the ground to a height of 2 m. What is its gravitational potential energy relative to the ground?

Solution
\(U_g = mgh = (5\,\text{kg})(9.8\,\text{m/s}^2)(2\,\text{m}) = 98\,\text{J}\)
Practice Problem

A 60 kg person climbs a flight of stairs, rising 3 m vertically. How much gravitational potential energy do they gain?

Solution
\(\Delta U_g = mg\Delta h = (60\,\text{kg})(9.8\,\text{m/s}^2)(3\,\text{m}) = 1764\,\text{J}\)

6.2 Conservation of Energy

The principle of conservation of energy is one of the most fundamental and powerful concepts in physics.

Definition

Conservation of Mechanical Energy: When only conservative forces act on a system, the total mechanical energy remains constant:

\(E_i = E_f \quad \text{or} \quad K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f\)

where:

  • \(E\) = total mechanical energy
  • \(K\) = kinetic energy
  • \(U\) = potential energy (gravitational + elastic)
Key Concept

Key points about energy conservation:

  • Conservative forces (gravity, spring force) conserve mechanical energy
  • Non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance) convert mechanical energy to other forms
  • The total energy in an isolated system is always conserved
  • Energy can be transformed from one form to another but cannot be created or destroyed
Types of Forces
  • Conservative forces: Work done is path-independent; mechanical energy is conserved
    • Gravity
    • Spring force
    • Electric force
  • Non-conservative forces: Work done depends on path; mechanical energy is not conserved
    • Friction
    • Air resistance
    • Tension (when it causes deformation)
Figure 6.4: Work as the area under the force-displacement curve
Example

A 2 kg object is dropped from a height of 10 m. What is its speed just before it hits the ground? (Ignore air resistance)

Solution

Using conservation of energy:

\(K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f\)
\(0 + mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 + 0\)
\((2)(9.8)(10) = \frac{1}{2}(2)v_f^2\)
\(196 = v_f^2 \;\Rightarrow\; v_f = \sqrt{196} = 14\,\text{m/s}\)
Example

A 10 kg object slides from rest down a frictionless incline that is 5 m above the ground. What is its speed at the bottom of the incline? (Ignore friction and air resistance.)

Solution

Because the incline is frictionless, mechanical energy is conserved. The initial kinetic energy is zero and the initial potential energy is \(mgh\), while at the bottom all of it is kinetic:

\(mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2\)

The mass cancels, giving

\(v_f = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2(9.8)(5)} = \sqrt{98} \approx 9.9\,\text{m/s}\)

The final speed is about \(9.9\,\text{m/s}\), independent of the mass of the object. This is the same result one would get from the constant-acceleration kinematic equation \(v^2 = 2gh\).

Practice Problem

A 0.5 kg ball is thrown vertically upward with an initial speed of 20 m/s. What maximum height does it reach?

Solution

Using conservation of energy:

\(K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f\)
\(\frac{1}{2}mv_i^2 + 0 = 0 + mgh_{\text{max}}\)
\(\frac{1}{2}(0.5)(20)^2 = (0.5)(9.8)h_{\text{max}}\)
\(100 = 4.9h_{\text{max}} \;\Rightarrow\; h_{\text{max}} = \frac{100}{4.9} \approx 20.41\,\text{m}\)
Problem-Solving Strategy for Energy Conservation
  1. Identify the system and all forces acting on it
  2. Determine if mechanical energy is conserved (only conservative forces)
  3. Choose reference points for potential energy
  4. Write the energy conservation equation: \(K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f\)
  5. Solve for the unknown quantity
  6. Check if your answer makes physical sense

When non-conservative forces act, the energy statement becomes

\(K_i + U_i + W_{\text{nc}} = K_f + U_f\)

where \(W_{\text{nc}}\) is the work done by non-conservative forces such as friction. Because kinetic friction opposes the motion, \(W_{\text{nc}}\) is usually negative and the final mechanical energy is less than the initial mechanical energy. The missing energy appears as thermal energy in the surfaces.

Example

A \(5.0\,\text{kg}\) block slides from rest down a rough ramp. Its starting height is \(4.0\,\text{m}\) above the bottom, and friction does \(-60\,\text{J}\) of work during the slide. Find the block's speed at the bottom.

Solution

Take the bottom of the ramp as \(h = 0\). Initially \(K_i = 0\) and \(U_i = mgh\). At the bottom \(U_f = 0\), so

\(mgh + W_{\text{nc}} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_f^2\)
\((5.0)(9.8)(4.0) - 60 = \tfrac{1}{2}(5.0)v_f^2\)
\(196 - 60 = 136 = 2.5v_f^2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad v_f = \sqrt{54.4} \approx 7.4\,\text{m/s}\)

Without friction the speed would have been \(\sqrt{2gh} = 8.9\,\text{m/s}\), so friction removed a significant fraction of the mechanical energy.

6.3 Power

Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

Definition

Average power is the work done divided by the time interval:

\(P_{\text{avg}} = \frac{W}{\Delta t}\)

Instantaneous power is the derivative of work with respect to time:

\(P = \frac{dW}{dt}\)

For a constant force, power can also be expressed as:

\(P = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{v} = Fv\cos\theta\)

where:

  • \(P\) = power (watts, W)
  • \(W\) = work (joules, J)
  • \(t\) = time (seconds, s)
  • \(F\) = force (newtons, N)
  • \(v\) = velocity (meters per second, m/s)

1 watt = 1 joule/second (1 W = 1 J/s)

Key Concept

Key points about power:

  • Power is a scalar quantity
  • Power describes how quickly work is done or energy is transferred
  • Mechanical power can be calculated as force times velocity
  • Efficiency relates useful power output to total power input
Example

A motor lifts a 100 kg crate vertically at a constant speed of 2 m/s. What power does the motor deliver?

Solution

The force needed equals the weight of the crate:

\(F = mg = (100\,\text{kg})(9.8\,\text{m/s}^2) = 980\,\text{N}\)
\(P = Fv = (980\,\text{N})(2\,\text{m/s}) = 1960\,\text{W}\)
Practice Problem

A person does 5000 J of work in 10 seconds. What is their average power output?

Solution
\(P_{\text{avg}} = \frac{W}{\Delta t} = \frac{5000\,\text{J}}{10\,\text{s}} = 500\,\text{W}\)
Example

A motor with an electrical power input of \(2.5\,\text{kW}\) lifts a \(200\,\text{kg}\) load at a constant speed of \(1.0\,\text{m/s}\). Find the useful mechanical power and the efficiency of the motor.

Solution

The useful power is the rate at which the motor does work against gravity:

\(P_{\text{useful}} = Fv = (mg)v = (200)(9.8)(1.0) = 1960\,\text{W}\)

The efficiency is the ratio of useful output to total input:

\(\eta = \frac{P_{\text{useful}}}{P_{\text{input}}} = \frac{1960}{2500} = 0.784 \approx 78\%\)

The remaining \(22\%\) is lost mainly as heat in the motor and gearbox.

Problem-Solving Workshop

Energy methods turn a complicated force problem into a bookkeeping exercise. Begin by defining the system and the two states you will compare: the block at the top of the incline and the block at the instant the spring reaches maximum compression. Then account for every energy transfer across the system boundary.

Friction is the key complication here. Gravity and the spring force are conservative and can be represented by potential energy, but kinetic friction removes mechanical energy. Compute the friction force from the normal force on the incline, find the (negative) work it does over the slide, and include that term explicitly rather than assuming mechanical energy is conserved.

Problem-Solving Checklist
  1. Define the system and identify the initial and final states.
  2. Sketch the incline and spring, and choose a reference height where \(h = 0\).
  3. List every force that does work; classify gravity and spring forces as conservative and friction as non-conservative.
  4. On the incline, use \(N = mg\cos\theta\) and \(f_k = \mu_k N\) to find the friction force.
  5. Write \(K_i + U_i + W_{\text{nc}} = K_f + U_f\), with \(W_{\text{nc}} = -f_k L\) and \(K_f = 0\) at maximum compression.
  6. Solve for the unknown and check its sign, units, and physical size.
Worked Example

A 2.0 kg block starts from rest at the top of a rough incline that is 6.0 m long and makes an angle of \(30^\circ\) with the horizontal. The coefficient of kinetic friction between the block and the incline is 0.25. At the bottom, the block compresses a spring with \(k = 800\,\text{N/m}\). The short horizontal section leading to the spring is frictionless. Find the maximum compression of the spring.

Solution

Take the bottom of the incline as the reference level \(h = 0\). The initial height is:

\(h = L\sin 30^\circ = (6.0\,\text{m})(0.500) = 3.0\,\text{m}\)

The normal force on the incline is \(N = mg\cos 30^\circ\), so the kinetic friction force is:

\(f_k = \mu_k mg\cos 30^\circ = (0.25)(2.0)(9.8)(0.866) = 4.24\,\text{N}\)

Friction acts opposite to the motion along the 6.0 m incline, so its work is:

\(W_{\text{nc}} = -f_k L = -(4.24\,\text{N})(6.0\,\text{m}) = -25.5\,\text{J}\)

At the top, \(K_i = 0\) and \(U_i = mgh\). At maximum compression, the block is momentarily at rest, so \(K_f = 0\), and all remaining energy is stored in the spring. Thus:

\(mgh + W_{\text{nc}} = \frac{1}{2}kx^2\)
\((2.0)(9.8)(3.0) - 25.5 = \frac{1}{2}(800)x^2\)
\(58.8 - 25.5 = 33.3 = 400x^2\)
\(x = \sqrt{\frac{33.3}{400}} = \sqrt{0.0833} \approx 0.29\,\text{m}\)

The maximum compression is therefore about \(0.29\,\text{m}\), or \(29\,\text{cm}\). This is less than the 6.0 m incline length, so the assumed geometry is consistent.

Why This Matters

Crash attenuators, railway buffers, and roller-coaster brakes all use springs or crushable materials to absorb energy over a controlled distance. Engineers combine the work done by friction with stored spring energy to predict stopping distances and to ensure that a vehicle or rider comes to rest safely rather than abruptly.

Key Takeaways

  • Work is energy transferred by a force, and for a constant force \(W = Fd\cos\theta\).
  • The net work on an object equals its change in kinetic energy, \(W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K\).
  • Gravity and ideal springs are conservative and can be described by potential energy.
  • Kinetic friction is non-conservative; it removes mechanical energy and usually does negative work.
  • Mechanical energy is conserved only when the work done by non-conservative forces is zero.
  • Gravitational and elastic potential energies are \(U_g = mgh\) and \(U_s = \frac{1}{2}kx^2\).
  • Power measures the rate of energy transfer, \(P = W/\Delta t = Fv\cos\theta\).
