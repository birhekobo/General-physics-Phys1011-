Chapter 7: Linear Momentum

Welcome to Linear Momentum

Linear momentum is the quantity that classical physics says is conserved when no net external force acts. It is defined as the product of mass and velocity: \(\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\), and it is a vector whose direction follows the velocity.

The impulse-momentum theorem connects a force acting over time to a change in momentum: \(\vec{J} = \Delta\vec{p} = \vec{p}_f - \vec{p}_i\). Together with Newton's second law, this framework makes collisions, explosions, and rocket motion exercises in bookkeeping rather than in repeated force calculations.

This chapter develops momentum conservation, collision analysis, and the center of mass, providing the bookkeeping tools used later in gravity, energy, and electromagnetism.

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Understand the concept of momentum and impulse
  • Apply the impulse-momentum theorem to solve problems
  • Understand and apply the principle of conservation of momentum
  • Analyze different types of collisions (elastic, inelastic, perfectly inelastic)
  • Solve problems involving one-dimensional and two-dimensional collisions
  • Understand the concept of center of mass and its calculation
  • Apply momentum conservation to complex systems
  • Analyze rocket propulsion using momentum principles

7.1 The Concept of Momentum and Impulse

Definition

Linear momentum of an object is defined as the product of its mass and velocity:

\(\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\)

where:

  • \(\vec{p}\) = momentum (kg·m/s)
  • \(m\) = mass (kg)
  • \(\vec{v}\) = velocity (m/s)

Momentum is a vector quantity with the same direction as the velocity.

Key Concept

Key points about momentum:

  • Momentum depends on both mass and velocity
  • A large mass moving slowly can have the same momentum as a small mass moving fast
  • Momentum is conserved in isolated systems
  • The rate of change of momentum equals the net force applied (Newton's second law)
Definition

Impulse is defined as the product of force and the time interval during which the force acts:

\(\vec{J} = \vec{F}\Delta t\)

where:

  • \(\vec{J}\) = impulse (N·s)
  • \(\vec{F}\) = average force (N)
  • \(\Delta t\) = time interval (s)

Note: 1 N·s = 1 kg·m/s (same units as momentum)

Impulse-Momentum Theorem

Impulse-Momentum Theorem: The impulse on an object equals its change in momentum:

\(\vec{J} = \Delta\vec{p} = \vec{p}_f - \vec{p}_i\)

This is equivalent to Newton's second law: \(\vec{F} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt}\)

Figure 7.1: Graphical representation of impulse

For a force that varies with time, the impulse is the integral of the force over the interval:

\(\vec{J} = \int_{t_i}^{t_f}\vec{F}\,dt\)

Graphically, this is the area under the force-time curve. The average force is then defined so that the rectangular area \(F_{\text{avg}}\Delta t\) equals the actual impulse:

\(F_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t_i}^{t_f}F\,dt\)

This is why airbags and crumple zones work: they extend the collision time, reducing the average force for the same change in momentum.

Example

A 0.5 kg ball moving at 4 m/s is struck by a bat. After the impact, it moves at 6 m/s in the opposite direction. If the contact time is 0.02 s, what is the average force exerted by the bat?

Solution
\(\Delta p = m(v_f - v_i) = 0.5(-6 - 4) = 0.5(-10) = -5\,\text{kg·m/s}\)
\(J = \Delta p = -5\,\text{N·s}\)
\(F = \frac{J}{\Delta t} = \frac{-5}{0.02} = -250\,\text{N}\)

The negative sign indicates the force is opposite to the initial velocity.

Practice Problem

A 1000 kg car accelerates from 20 m/s to 30 m/s in 5 seconds. What is the average force required?

Solution
\(\Delta p = m(v_f - v_i) = 1000(30 - 20) = 10{,}000\,\text{kg·m/s}\)
\(F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \frac{10{,}000}{5} = 2{,}000\,\text{N}\)

7.2 Conservation of Momentum

Definition

Conservation of Momentum: In an isolated system (no external forces), the total momentum remains constant:

\(\sum\vec{p}_{\text{initial}} = \sum\vec{p}_{\text{final}}\)

or

\(m_1\vec{v}_{1i} + m_2\vec{v}_{2i} = m_1\vec{v}_{1f} + m_2\vec{v}_{2f}\)
Key Concept

Key points about momentum conservation:

  • Momentum is conserved in all directions separately
  • The system must be isolated (no net external force)
  • Internal forces (between objects in the system) don't affect the total momentum
  • Momentum conservation applies to both elastic and inelastic collisions
Figure 7.2: Conservation of momentum in collisions
Example

A 2 kg object moving at 3 m/s collides with a stationary 3 kg object. After the collision, the 2 kg object moves at 1 m/s. What is the velocity of the 3 kg object?

Solution

Using conservation of momentum:

\(m_1v_{1i} + m_2v_{2i} = m_1v_{1f} + m_2v_{2f}\)
\((2)(3) + (3)(0) = (2)(1) + (3)v_{2f}\)
\(6 = 2 + 3v_{2f}\)
\(3v_{2f} = 4 \;\Rightarrow\; v_{2f} = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.33\,\text{m/s}\)
Practice Problem

A 0.1 kg bullet is fired into a 2 kg block at rest on a frictionless surface. The bullet remains in the block, and they move together at 4 m/s. What was the bullet's initial speed?

Solution
\(m_bv_{bi} + m_{bl}v_{bli} = (m_b + m_{bl})v_f\)
\((0.1)v_{bi} + (2)(0) = (2.1)(4)\)
\(0.1v_{bi} = 8.4 \;\Rightarrow\; v_{bi} = 84\,\text{m/s}\)
Example

A \(4.0\,\text{kg}\) rifle fires a \(0.020\,\text{kg}\) bullet at \(600\,\text{m/s}\). Find the recoil speed of the rifle and explain why the rifle does not hurt the shoulder as much as the bullet hurts its target.

Solution

Take the bullet's direction as positive. The system is initially at rest, so

\(0 = m_bv_b + m_rv_r\)
\(v_r = -\frac{m_b}{m_r}v_b = -\frac{0.020}{4.0}(600) = -3.0\,\text{m/s}\)

The rifle recoils at \(3.0\,\text{m/s}\) in the opposite direction. The momentum magnitudes are equal, but the rifle's much larger mass means a much smaller speed. The rifle is also held against the shoulder for a relatively long time, so the average force on the shoulder is smaller than the force the bullet experiences over its short stopping time.

Example

A 2 kg ball moving at \(4\,\text{m/s}\) collides inelastically with a 3 kg ball moving at \(2\,\text{m/s}\) in the opposite direction. Find the velocity of the combined mass after the collision.

Solution

For a perfectly inelastic collision, the objects stick together, so the final velocity is a single unknown:

\(m_1v_1 + m_2v_2 = (m_1 + m_2)v_f\)
\((2)(4) + (3)(-2) = (5)v_f\)
\(8 - 6 = 5v_f \;\Rightarrow\; v_f = \frac{2}{5} = 0.4\,\text{m/s}\)

The combined mass moves at \(0.4\,\text{m/s}\) in the original direction of the 2 kg ball. Momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is not: part of it was converted into heat, sound, and deformation during the collision.

7.3 Collisions in One Dimension and in Two Dimensions

7.3.1 Perfectly Inelastic Collisions

Definition

Perfectly Inelastic Collision: A collision where the objects stick together after impact. Maximum kinetic energy is lost.

\(m_1\vec{v}_{1i} + m_2\vec{v}_{2i} = (m_1 + m_2)\vec{v}_f\)

Kinetic energy is not conserved: \(K_f < K_i\)

Key Concept

Characteristics of perfectly inelastic collisions:

  • Objects move together with common velocity after collision
  • Maximum possible kinetic energy is lost
  • Momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is not
  • Some kinetic energy is converted to other forms (heat, sound, deformation)
Example

A 1000 kg car moving at 20 m/s collides with a stationary 1500 kg car. If they stick together, what is their final velocity?

Solution
\(m_1v_{1i} + m_2v_{2i} = (m_1 + m_2)v_f\)
\((1000)(20) + (1500)(0) = (2500)v_f\)
\(20{,}000 = 2500v_f \;\Rightarrow\; v_f = 8\,\text{m/s}\)

7.3.2 Elastic Collisions

Definition

Elastic Collision: A collision where both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved.

\(m_1\vec{v}_{1i} + m_2\vec{v}_{2i} = m_1\vec{v}_{1f} + m_2\vec{v}_{2f}\)
\(\frac{1}{2}m_1v_{1i}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2v_{2i}^2 = \frac{1}{2}m_1v_{1f}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2v_{2f}^2\)
Special Cases for Elastic Collisions
  • Equal masses: Objects exchange velocities
  • Stationary target:
    \(v_{1f} = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i}, \quad v_{2f} = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i}\)
  • Heavy target (\(m_2 \gg m_1\)): Light object rebounds with nearly same speed
  • Light target (\(m_2 \ll m_1\)): Heavy object continues almost unaffected
Example

A 2 kg object moving at 4 m/s collides elastically with a stationary 1 kg object. Find their final velocities.

Solution

Using the formulas for stationary target:

\(v_{1f} = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i} = \frac{2 - 1}{2 + 1}(4) = \frac{1}{3}(4) = \frac{4}{3}\,\text{m/s}\)
\(v_{2f} = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i} = \frac{4}{2 + 1}(4) = \frac{4}{3}(4) = \frac{16}{3}\,\text{m/s}\)

7.3.3 Two Dimensional (Glancing) Collisions

Definition

Two-Dimensional Collisions: Collisions where objects don't move along a single line. Both x and y components of momentum must be conserved separately.

\(\sum p_{x,\text{initial}} = \sum p_{x,\text{final}}\)
\(\sum p_{y,\text{initial}} = \sum p_{y,\text{final}}\)
Key Concept

For two-dimensional collisions:

  • Momentum is conserved in both x and y directions separately
  • Usually need additional information (angles, one final velocity, etc.)
  • Set up coordinate system and resolve velocities into components
  • Solve the system of equations
Figure 7.3: Glancing collision with velocity components
Example

Two identical balls collide. One was initially stationary. After collision, the first ball moves at 30° to its original direction. If the collision is elastic, find the directions and speeds.

Solution

For elastic collision of equal masses with one stationary:

\(v_{1f} = v_{1i}\cos\theta, \quad v_{2f} = v_{1i}\sin\theta\)
\(\theta + \phi = 90^\circ\)

Given \(\theta = 30^\circ\), then \(\phi = 60^\circ\)

\(v_{1f} = v_{1i}\cos 30^\circ = 0.866v_{1i}\)
\(v_{2f} = v_{1i}\sin 30^\circ = 0.5v_{1i}\)

7.3.4 Rocket Propulsion

A rocket is a dramatic application of momentum conservation. The rocket and its exhaust gases form an isolated system: the momentum gained by the rocket is equal and opposite to the momentum carried away by the gas. If the engine ejects mass at a rate \(dm/dt\) with exhaust speed \(v_{\text{ex}}\) relative to the rocket, the thrust is

\(F_{\text{thrust}} = v_{\text{ex}}\frac{dm}{dt}\)

This thrust does not require anything to push against; the rocket pushes on its own exhaust. As fuel is burned, the rocket's mass decreases, so its acceleration increases even if the thrust stays constant. This is why the rocket equation is logarithmic rather than linear, although a full derivation is beyond this chapter.

Example

A rocket engine ejects gas at \(2.5 \times 10^3\,\text{m/s}\) relative to the rocket at a rate of \(80\,\text{kg/s}\). Find the thrust. If the rocket's mass is \(4.0 \times 10^3\,\text{kg}\), find its initial acceleration.

Solution

The thrust is

\(F_{\text{thrust}} = v_{\text{ex}}\frac{dm}{dt} = (2.5 \times 10^3)(80) = 2.0 \times 10^5\,\text{N}\)

The net force at liftoff is thrust minus weight:

\(F_{\text{net}} = 2.0 \times 10^5 - (4.0 \times 10^3)(9.8) = 2.0 \times 10^5 - 3.92 \times 10^4 = 1.61 \times 10^5\,\text{N}\)

Thus

\(a = \frac{F_{\text{net}}}{m} = \frac{1.61 \times 10^5}{4.0 \times 10^3} \approx 40\,\text{m/s}^2\)

7.4 The Concept of Center of Mass

Definition

Center of Mass: The point where the total mass of a system can be considered to be concentrated. For a system of particles:

\(\vec{r}_{CM} = \frac{\sum m_i\vec{r}_i}{\sum m_i} = \frac{m_1\vec{r}_1 + m_2\vec{r}_2 + \cdots + m_n\vec{r}_n}{m_1 + m_2 + \cdots + m_n}\)

In component form:

\(x_{CM} = \frac{\sum m_ix_i}{\sum m_i}, \quad y_{CM} = \frac{\sum m_iy_i}{\sum m_i}, \quad z_{CM} = \frac{\sum m_iz_i}{\sum m_i}\)
Key Concept

Properties of center of mass:

  • The center of mass moves as if all mass were concentrated there
  • External forces accelerate the center of mass as if it were a point particle
  • Internal forces don't affect the motion of the center of mass
  • The center of mass may not coincide with any actual mass point
Center of Mass Velocity and Acceleration
\(\vec{v}_{CM} = \frac{\sum m_i\vec{v}_i}{\sum m_i}, \quad \vec{a}_{CM} = \frac{\sum m_i\vec{a}_i}{\sum m_i} = \frac{\sum \vec{F}_{ext}}{M}\)

The center of mass accelerates according to Newton's second law with all external forces.

Figure 7.4: Center of mass of a system of particles
Example

Find the center of mass of a system with a 2 kg mass at (1,2) and a 3 kg mass at (4,1).

Solution
\(x_{CM} = \frac{(2)(1) + (3)(4)}{2 + 3} = \frac{2 + 12}{5} = \frac{14}{5} = 2.8\)
\(y_{CM} = \frac{(2)(2) + (3)(1)}{2 + 3} = \frac{4 + 3}{5} = \frac{7}{5} = 1.4\)

Center of mass is at (2.8, 1.4)

Practice Problem

A 1 kg mass is at (0,0), a 2 kg mass at (3,0), and a 3 kg mass at (0,4). Find the center of mass.

Solution
\(x_{CM} = \frac{(1)(0) + (2)(3) + (3)(0)}{1 + 2 + 3} = \frac{0 + 6 + 0}{6} = 1\)
\(y_{CM} = \frac{(1)(0) + (2)(0) + (3)(4)}{6} = \frac{0 + 0 + 12}{6} = 2\)

Center of mass is at (1, 2)

For a uniform symmetric body, the center of mass lies at its geometric centre: the midpoint of a rod, the centre of a disk or sphere, and the intersection of the medians for a triangular plate. For a composite body, treat each part as a point mass located at that part's own centre of mass and apply the same weighted-average formula.

The center of mass is especially useful because it obeys Newton's second law for the system as a whole:

\(\vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = M\vec{a}_{CM}\)

Internal forces, however violent, cannot change the motion of the centre of mass. In an explosion, for example, the fragments fly apart but their centre of mass continues along the same path it would have followed without the explosion.

Example

A uniform \(2.0\,\text{m}\) board of mass \(6.0\,\text{kg}\) rests horizontally. A \(4.0\,\text{kg}\) mass is placed at one end. Find the center of mass of the combined system, measured from that end.

Solution

The board's centre of mass is at its midpoint, \(1.0\,\text{m}\) from the end. Treat the board and the added mass as two point masses:

\(x_{CM} = \frac{m_{\text{board}}x_{\text{board}} + m_{\text{mass}}x_{\text{mass}}}{m_{\text{board}} + m_{\text{mass}}} = \frac{(6.0)(1.0) + (4.0)(0)}{10.0} = 0.60\,\text{m}\)

The combined centre of mass is \(0.60\,\text{m}\) from the loaded end, closer to the heavier board's midpoint than to the added mass.

Problem-Solving Workshop

Momentum is a vector, so a two-dimensional collision cannot be handled by a single equation. Set up \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes, write the initial momentum components, and require each component to be conserved separately. For a perfectly inelastic collision, the objects share one final velocity vector.

After finding that velocity, treat kinetic energy separately. Momentum conservation does not imply kinetic energy conservation; in a perfectly inelastic collision the missing kinetic energy has been converted into deformation, heat, and sound. Calculate \(K_i\) and \(K_f\) explicitly and report the difference.

Problem-Solving Checklist
  1. Choose the system and confirm that external impulses during the collision are negligible.
  2. Draw the "before" and "after" situation and label a convenient \(x\)-\(y\) coordinate system.
  3. Resolve every initial velocity into \(x\)- and \(y\)-components.
  4. For a perfectly inelastic collision, set the final velocity common to both objects and write momentum conservation in each direction.
  5. Combine the final components to obtain the speed and the direction angle.
  6. Compute \(K_i\), \(K_f\), and the kinetic energy lost; interpret the loss as deformation, heat, and sound.
Worked Example

A 1200 kg car traveling east at \(20\,\text{m/s}\) collides at an intersection with a 1800 kg truck traveling north at \(15\,\text{m/s}\). The vehicles stick together after the collision. Find the velocity of the wreckage immediately afterward and the kinetic energy lost in the collision.

Solution

Take east as the \(+x\)-direction and north as the \(+y\)-direction. The initial momentum components are:

\(p_x = m_1v_{1x} + m_2v_{2x} = (1200)(20) + (1800)(0) = 2.40 \times 10^4\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}\)
\(p_y = m_1v_{1y} + m_2v_{2y} = (1200)(0) + (1800)(15) = 2.70 \times 10^4\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}\)

The combined mass is \(M = 1200 + 1800 = 3000\,\text{kg}\). Since the vehicles stick together, momentum conservation gives the final velocity components:

\(v_x = \frac{p_x}{M} = \frac{2.40 \times 10^4}{3000} = 8.0\,\text{m/s}\)
\(v_y = \frac{p_y}{M} = \frac{2.70 \times 10^4}{3000} = 9.0\,\text{m/s}\)

The final speed and direction are:

\(v_f = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2} = \sqrt{8.0^2 + 9.0^2} = \sqrt{145} \approx 12.0\,\text{m/s}\)
\(\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{9.0}{8.0}\right) \approx 48^\circ \text{ north of east}\)

The initial and final kinetic energies are:

\(K_i = \frac{1}{2}(1200)(20)^2 + \frac{1}{2}(1800)(15)^2 = 240{,}000 + 202{,}500 = 442{,}500\,\text{J}\)
\(K_f = \frac{1}{2}(3000)(\sqrt{145})^2 = \frac{1}{2}(3000)(145) = 217{,}500\,\text{J}\)
\(\Delta K = K_f - K_i = 217{,}500 - 442{,}500 = -225{,}000\,\text{J}\)

About \(2.25 \times 10^5\,\text{J}\) of kinetic energy is lost, roughly 51% of the initial kinetic energy. That energy appears as deformation of the vehicles, heat, and sound.

Why This Matters

Accident investigators use momentum components and skid-mark directions to reconstruct intersection collisions and estimate impact speeds. The same vector bookkeeping is used in spacecraft docking, where two craft must match both speed and direction before they latch together.

Key Takeaways

  • Momentum is a vector, \(\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\), so direction matters.
  • Impulse equals the change in momentum, \(\vec{J} = \vec{F}\Delta t = \Delta\vec{p}\).
  • In an isolated system, total momentum is conserved in every direction separately.
  • In a perfectly inelastic collision, the objects stick together and share one final velocity.
  • Momentum is conserved in all collisions, but kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions.
  • Two-dimensional collisions require separate \(x\)- and \(y\)-momentum equations.
  • The center of mass moves as though all external forces acted at that point.

Summary

Key Concepts from Chapter 7
  • Momentum: \(\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\) (kg·m/s)
  • Impulse: \(\vec{J} = \vec{F}\Delta t = \Delta\vec{p}\) (N·s)
  • Conservation of momentum: \(\sum\vec{p}_{\text{initial}} = \sum\vec{p}_{\text{final}}\) (isolated systems)
  • Perfectly inelastic collisions: Objects stick together, max KE loss
  • Elastic collisions: Both momentum and KE conserved
  • Center of mass: \(\vec{r}_{CM} = \frac{\sum m_i\vec{r}_i}{\sum m_i}\)
  • CM motion: \(\vec{a}_{CM} = \frac{\sum \vec{F}_{ext}}{M}\)
