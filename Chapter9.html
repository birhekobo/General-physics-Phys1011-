Chapter 9: Heat and Thermodynamics

Welcome to Heat and Thermodynamics

Heat and thermodynamics describe how energy moves and transforms when thermal effects are involved. Temperature tells us how hot a system is, but heat is the actual transfer of energy between systems at different temperatures.

The concepts of specific heat and latent heat let us calculate the energy required to raise temperature or cause phase changes, while the First Law of Thermodynamics, \( \Delta U = Q - W \), puts thermal processes on an energy balance sheet.

Combined with the mechanisms of heat transfer — conduction, convection, and radiation — this chapter gives a complete picture of how thermal energy behaves in nature and technology.

9.1 The Concept of Temperature: Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics

Definition

Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance. It determines the direction of heat flow between objects.

Definition

Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics: If two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, then they are in thermal equilibrium with each other.

This law establishes temperature as a fundamental property and allows for temperature measurement using thermometers.

Key Concept

Key points about temperature and thermal equilibrium:

  • Objects in thermal equilibrium have the same temperature
  • Heat flows from higher temperature to lower temperature
  • The Zeroth Law justifies the use of thermometers
  • Thermal equilibrium does not mean no energy exchange, but rather no net energy exchange
Figure 9.1: Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics
Example

If thermometer A reads the same temperature when in contact with object B and when in contact with object C, what can we conclude about the temperatures of B and C?

Solution

According to the Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics, if thermometer A is in thermal equilibrium with both B and C, then B and C are in thermal equilibrium with each other and have the same temperature.

9.2 Temperature Scales and Absolute Temperature

Definition

The three common temperature scales are:

  • Celsius (°C): Based on water's freezing (0°C) and boiling (100°C) points
  • Fahrenheit (°F): Used primarily in the United States
  • Kelvin (K): The SI unit of temperature, with absolute zero at 0 K
Definition

Absolute zero is the theoretical temperature at which all molecular motion ceases. It is 0 K on the Kelvin scale, which is approximately \(-273.15\,^\circ\text{C}\).

Temperature Conversion Formulas
\(T_K = T_C + 273.15\)
\(T_C = \frac{5}{9}(T_F - 32)\)
\(T_F = \frac{9}{5}T_C + 32\)
Figure 9.2: Kelvin and Celsius temperature scales
Example

Convert 25°C to Kelvin and Fahrenheit.

Solution

To Kelvin:

\(T_K = T_C + 273.15 = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15\,\text{K}\)

To Fahrenheit:

\(T_F = \frac{9}{5}T_C + 32 = \frac{9}{5}(25) + 32 = 45 + 32 = 77\,^\circ\text{F}\)
Practice Problem

Convert 98.6°F (human body temperature) to Celsius and Kelvin.

Solution

To Celsius:

\(T_C = \frac{5}{9}(T_F - 32) = \frac{5}{9}(98.6 - 32) = \frac{5}{9}(66.6) = 37\,^\circ\text{C}\)

To Kelvin:

\(T_K = T_C + 273.15 = 37 + 273.15 = 310.15\,\text{K}\)

9.3 Thermal Expansion

Definition

Thermal expansion is the tendency of matter to change its volume in response to temperature changes.

9.3.1 Linear Expansion

Definition

Linear expansion describes the change in length of an object with temperature:

\(\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T\)

where:

  • \(\Delta L\) = change in length
  • \(\alpha\) = coefficient of linear expansion
  • \(L_0\) = original length
  • \(\Delta T\) = change in temperature

9.3.2 Volume Expansion

Definition

Volume expansion describes the change in volume of an object with temperature:

\(\Delta V = \beta V_0 \Delta T\)

where:

  • \(\Delta V\) = change in volume
  • \(\beta\) = coefficient of volume expansion (\(\beta \approx 3\alpha\) for isotropic materials)
  • \(V_0\) = original volume
  • \(\Delta T\) = change in temperature
Key Concept

Key points about thermal expansion:

  • Different materials have different expansion coefficients
  • Bimetallic strips use differential expansion for temperature measurement
  • Expansion joints in bridges and buildings accommodate thermal expansion
  • Water is unusual because it expands when freezing
Figure 9.3: Thermal expansion of a solid object
Example

An aluminum rod is 2.0 m long at 20°C. If the coefficient of linear expansion for aluminum is \(24 \times 10^{-6}/^\circ\text{C}\), how much does it expand when heated to 100°C?

Solution
\(\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T = (24 \times 10^{-6})(2.0)(100 - 20) = (24 \times 10^{-6})(2.0)(80) = 3.84 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m} = 3.84\,\text{mm}\)
Practice Problem

A glass flask has a volume of 500 mL at 20°C. If the coefficient of volume expansion for glass is \(27 \times 10^{-6}/^\circ\text{C}\), what is its volume at 80°C?

Solution
\(\Delta V = \beta V_0 \Delta T = (27 \times 10^{-6})(500)(80 - 20) = (27 \times 10^{-6})(500)(60) = 0.81\,\text{mL}\)
\(V = V_0 + \Delta V = 500 + 0.81 = 500.81\,\text{mL}\)

9.4 The Concept of Heat and Energy

Definition

Heat is the transfer of thermal energy between objects due to a temperature difference. It flows from hotter objects to cooler objects.

Definition

Internal energy is the total energy contained within a system, including both kinetic and potential energy of its molecules.

Definition

Thermal equilibrium occurs when two objects in contact reach the same temperature and no net heat flows between them.

Key Concept

Key distinctions:

  • Temperature measures average kinetic energy per molecule
  • Heat is energy transferred due to temperature difference
  • Internal energy is the total energy of all molecules
  • Objects contain internal energy, not heat
Heat vs. Temperature
Heat Temperature
Form of energy Measure of hotness or coldness
Measured in joules (J) Measured in Kelvin (K), Celsius (°C), or Fahrenheit (°F)
Depends on mass and material Independent of mass and material
Transferred between systems Not transferred, but determines heat flow direction

9.5 Specific Heat and Latent Heat

Definition

Specific heat capacity (\(c\)) is the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1°C (or 1 K):

\(Q = mc\Delta T\)

where:

  • \(Q\) = heat transferred (joules, J)
  • \(m\) = mass (kilograms, kg)
  • \(c\) = specific heat capacity (J/kg·K)
  • \(\Delta T\) = temperature change (K or °C)
Definition

Latent heat is the heat required for a phase change at constant temperature:

\(Q = mL\)

where:

  • \(Q\) = heat transferred (joules, J)
  • \(m\) = mass (kilograms, kg)
  • \(L\) = latent heat (J/kg)
Key Concept

Key points about specific heat and latent heat:

  • Different phases have different specific heat capacities
  • Latent heat of fusion (\(L_f\)) is for solid-liquid transitions
  • Latent heat of vaporization (\(L_v\)) is for liquid-gas transitions
  • During phase changes, temperature remains constant despite heat input
Figure 9.4: Heating curve showing specific heat and latent heat regions
Example

How much heat is required to raise the temperature of 2 kg of water from 20°C to 80°C? (Specific heat of water = 4186 J/kg·K)

Solution
\(Q = mc\Delta T = (2)(4186)(80 - 20) = (2)(4186)(60) = 502{,}320\,\text{J} = 502.32\,\text{kJ}\)
Practice Problem

How much heat is required to melt 0.5 kg of ice at 0°C? (Latent heat of fusion for ice = 334,000 J/kg)

Solution
\(Q = mL_f = (0.5)(334{,}000) = 167{,}000\,\text{J} = 167\,\text{kJ}\)
Example

In a calorimetry experiment, 0.4 kg of water at 80°C is mixed with 0.2 kg of water at 20°C. Assuming no heat is lost to the surroundings, what is the final temperature of the mixture? (Specific heat of water = 4186 J/kg·K)

Solution

Heat lost by the hot water equals heat gained by the cold water:

\(m_h c (T_h - T_f) = m_c c (T_f - T_c)\)
\(0.4(80 - T_f) = 0.2(T_f - 20)\)
\(32 - 0.4T_f = 0.2T_f - 4 \;\Rightarrow\; 36 = 0.6T_f \;\Rightarrow\; T_f = 60\,\text{°C}\)

The final temperature of the mixture is 60°C.

9.6 Heat Transfer Mechanisms

Definition

Heat transfer mechanisms:

  • Conduction: Heat transfer through direct molecular contact
  • Convection: Heat transfer through fluid movement
  • Radiation: Heat transfer through electromagnetic waves

9.6.1 Conduction

Definition

Conduction is heat transfer through direct molecular contact. The rate of conductive heat transfer is given by:

\(\frac{Q}{t} = kA\frac{\Delta T}{d}\)

where:

  • \(Q/t\) = heat transfer rate (watts, W)
  • \(k\) = thermal conductivity (W/m·K)
  • \(A\) = cross-sectional area (m²)
  • \(\Delta T\) = temperature difference (K or °C)
  • \(d\) = thickness of the material (m)

9.6.2 Convection

Definition

Convection is heat transfer through the movement of fluids (liquids or gases). It occurs when warmer, less dense fluid rises and cooler, denser fluid sinks.

9.6.3 Radiation

Definition

Radiation is heat transfer through electromagnetic waves. All objects emit thermal radiation according to the Stefan-Boltzmann law:

\(P = \varepsilon\sigma A T^4\)

where:

  • \(P\) = radiated power (watts, W)
  • \(\varepsilon\) = emissivity (0 to 1, perfect blackbody = 1)
  • \(\sigma\) = Stefan-Boltzmann constant (\(5.67 \times 10^{-8}\,\text{W/m}^2\cdot\text{K}^4\))
  • \(A\) = surface area (m²)
  • \(T\) = absolute temperature (K)
Key Concept

Key points about heat transfer mechanisms:

  • Conduction works best in solids, especially metals
  • Convection requires fluid motion and is enhanced by density differences
  • Radiation can occur through vacuum and doesn't require a medium
  • Real-world heat transfer often involves multiple mechanisms
  • Insulators work by reducing all forms of heat transfer
Figure 9.5: The three mechanisms of heat transfer
Example

A copper rod (\(k = 400\,\text{W/m}\cdot\text{K}\)) with cross-sectional area 0.01 m² and length 0.5 m connects two reservoirs at 100°C and 20°C. What is the rate of heat conduction?

Solution
\(\frac{Q}{t} = kA\frac{\Delta T}{d} = (400)(0.01)\frac{(100 - 20)}{0.5} = (4)\frac{80}{0.5} = 4 \times 160 = 640\,\text{W}\)
Practice Problem

A blackbody sphere (emissivity = 1) with radius 0.1 m is at 500 K. How much power does it radiate?

Solution
\(A = 4\pi r^2 = 4\pi(0.1)^2 = 0.1257\,\text{m}^2\)
\(P = \varepsilon\sigma A T^4 = (1)(5.67 \times 10^{-8})(0.1257)(500)^4\)
\(= (1)(5.67 \times 10^{-8})(0.1257)(6.25 \times 10^{10}) = 444.7\,\text{W}\)
Example

A blackbody sphere of radius \(0.10\,\text{m}\) is at \(500\,\text{K}\) and is placed in surroundings at \(300\,\text{K}\). Find the net rate at which it loses energy by radiation.

Solution

The sphere both emits and absorbs radiation. Its surface area is \(A = 4\pi r^2 = 0.126\,\text{m}^2\), so the net radiated power is:

\(P_{\text{net}} = \varepsilon\sigma A\left(T^4 - T_{\text{sur}}^4\right)\)
\(P_{\text{net}} = (1)(5.67\times10^{-8})(0.126)\left(500^4 - 300^4\right)\)
\(= (7.14\times10^{-9})(6.25\times10^{10} - 8.1\times10^{9}) \approx 389\,\text{W}\)

The sphere therefore loses energy at a net rate of about \(389\,\text{W}\); it would cool unless energy were supplied.

9.7 First Law of Thermodynamics

Definition

First Law of Thermodynamics: The change in internal energy of a system equals the heat added to the system minus the work done by the system:

\(\Delta U = Q - W\)

where:

  • \(\Delta U\) = change in internal energy (J)
  • \(Q\) = heat added to the system (J)
  • \(W\) = work done by the system (J)
Key Concept

Key points about the First Law:

  • Energy is conserved in thermodynamic processes
  • Internal energy depends only on the state of the system
  • Heat and work are process-dependent, not state functions
  • The sign convention: \(Q > 0\) when heat enters, \(W > 0\) when work is done by the system
  • For ideal gases, internal energy depends only on temperature
First Law Sign Conventions
Quantity Positive Negative
Heat (\(Q\)) Added to system Removed from system
Work (\(W\)) Done by system Done on system
\(\Delta U\) Increases Decreases
Figure 9.6: First Law of Thermodynamics: energy balance for a system
Example

A system absorbs 500 J of heat and does 200 J of work on its surroundings. What is its change in internal energy?

Solution
\(\Delta U = Q - W = 500 - 200 = 300\,\text{J}\)

The internal energy increases by 300 J.

Practice Problem

If a system's internal energy decreases by 150 J while it does 100 J of work, how much heat is transferred?

Solution
\(\Delta U = Q - W \Rightarrow -150 = Q - 100 \Rightarrow Q = -150 + 100 = -50\,\text{J}\)

50 J of heat is removed from the system.

9.8 Applications of Thermodynamic Principles

Key Concept

Real-world applications of thermodynamics:

  • Refrigeration and air conditioning: Use phase changes and heat transfer to move thermal energy
  • Heat engines: Convert thermal energy to mechanical work (Carnot cycle, Otto cycle)
  • Building design: Insulation, windows, and ventilation systems optimize heat transfer
  • Meteorology: Atmospheric convection drives weather patterns
  • Medical applications: Fever measurement, cryotherapy, thermal imaging
  • Cooking and food preservation: Heat transfer controls chemical reactions
  • Energy production: Power plants use thermodynamic cycles
Example

Explain why a ceramic coffee mug feels warmer than a metal spoon at the same temperature.

Solution

Metal has higher thermal conductivity than ceramic. When you touch both objects at room temperature, heat flows from your hand more rapidly into the metal, making it feel colder. The ceramic mug, with lower conductivity, doesn't draw heat from your hand as quickly, so it feels warmer despite being at the same temperature.

Practice Problem

Why do bridges have expansion joints?

Solution

Bridges expand and contract with temperature changes due to thermal expansion. Without expansion joints, this would create enormous stresses that could damage the bridge structure. The joints allow the bridge to expand and contract safely.

Problem-Solving Workshop

Calorimetry problems are energy-conservation problems in disguise. For an insulated mixture, the heat lost by the warmer object equals the heat gained by the cooler object, provided no phase change occurs. Begin by listing masses, specific heats, and initial temperatures.

Use \(Q = mc\Delta T\) with a positive temperature difference on each side, or use explicit signs and set the algebraic sum of all heat transfers to zero. After solving for the final temperature, check that it lies between the initial temperatures and that no substance would melt or boil at that temperature.

Problem-Solving Checklist
  1. Identify the hot and cold objects, their masses, specific heats, and initial temperatures.
  2. Decide whether any phase change occurs; if not, use \(Q = mc\Delta T\) for each object.
  3. For an insulated container, write \(Q_{\text{lost}} = Q_{\text{gained}}\).
  4. Keep each temperature change positive on its own side so the signs do not cancel incorrectly.
  5. Solve the resulting linear equation for the final temperature.
  6. Check that the final temperature lies between the initial temperatures and that no phase change was missed.
Worked Example

A 0.50 kg copper block at \(200^\circ\text{C}\) is dropped into 0.80 kg of water at \(20^\circ\text{C}\) in an insulated container. Taking \(c_{\text{Cu}} = 387\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}\) and \(c_{\text{water}} = 4186\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}\), find the final temperature. Assume no heat loss to the surroundings and no boiling.

Solution

The copper cools while the water warms, so the heat lost by the copper equals the heat gained by the water:

\(m_{\text{Cu}}c_{\text{Cu}}(T_{\text{Cu}} - T_f) = m_{\text{water}}c_{\text{water}}(T_f - T_{\text{water}})\)

Substitute the known values:

\((0.50)(387)(200 - T_f) = (0.80)(4186)(T_f - 20)\)
\(193.5(200 - T_f) = 3348.8(T_f - 20)\)
\(38{,}700 - 193.5T_f = 3348.8T_f - 66{,}976\)

Collect the temperature terms on one side:

\(38{,}700 + 66{,}976 = 3348.8T_f + 193.5T_f\)
\(105{,}676 = 3542.3T_f\)
\(T_f = \frac{105{,}676}{3542.3} \approx 29.8^\circ\text{C}\)

The final temperature is about \(30^\circ\text{C}\), which lies between \(20^\circ\text{C}\) and \(200^\circ\text{C}\), so the assumption of no phase change is consistent. The copper transfers roughly \(3.3 \times 10^4\,\text{J}\) to the water.

Why This Matters

Calorimetry is used to measure the specific heats of materials, the energy content of foods and fuels, and the performance of engine coolants. The same heat balance guides the design of heat exchangers, thermal storage systems, and insulated buildings.

Example

A \(0.20\,\text{kg}\) block of ice at \(0^\circ\text{C}\) is added to \(1.0\,\text{kg}\) of water at \(30^\circ\text{C}\) in an insulated container. Find the final temperature. Use \(L_f = 3.34\times10^{5}\,\text{J/kg}\) and \(c_{\text{water}} = 4186\,\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}\).

Solution

First check whether the ice can be completely melted. The heat released when the water cools to \(0^\circ\text{C}\) is:

\(Q_{\text{available}} = (1.0)(4186)(30) \approx 1.26\times10^{5}\,\text{J}\)

The heat needed to melt all the ice is:

\(Q_{\text{melt}} = (0.20)(3.34\times10^{5}) = 6.68\times10^{4}\,\text{J}\)

Since \(Q_{\text{available}} > Q_{\text{melt}}\), all the ice melts and the final mixture is liquid water above \(0^\circ\text{C}\). Energy conservation gives:

\((1.0)(4186)(30 - T_f) = (0.20)(3.34\times10^{5}) + (0.20)(4186)(T_f - 0)\)
\(125{,}580 - 4186T_f = 66{,}800 + 837.2T_f\)
\(58{,}780 = 5023.2T_f \quad \Rightarrow \quad T_f \approx 11.7^\circ\text{C}\)

The final temperature is about \(12^\circ\text{C}\), consistent with all the ice having melted.

Key Takeaways

  • Temperature measures the average molecular kinetic energy and determines the direction of heat flow.
  • Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference; internal energy is the total molecular energy of a system.
  • The heat needed to change temperature without a phase change is \(Q = mc\Delta T\).
  • During a phase change at constant temperature, the heat transferred is \(Q = mL\).
  • In an insulated mixture, heat lost by warmer objects equals heat gained by cooler objects.
  • Conduction rate is \(Q/t = kA\Delta T/d\); convection and radiation are the other heat-transfer mechanisms.
  • The first law of thermodynamics, \(\Delta U = Q - W\), expresses energy conservation for a system.
